## 01 · [AI Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13928)

`alignment` `AI safety` `peer-reviewed` — Andis Draguns, Sumeet Ramesh Motwani, Raymond Douglas, Christian Schroeder de Witt — **NeurIPS 2026 (accepted)**

Capability evaluations are a cornerstone of deployment safety: if we can elicit and measure dangerous behaviours before release, we can decide whether a model is safe to deploy. This paper proves, via reduction to cryptography, that this strategy has a fundamental ceiling — a model can be engineered to conceal arbitrary capabilities from any elicitation procedure, including white-box methods with full weight access.

![Figure 1: Threat scenario — backdoor hides an encrypted arbitrary neural network inside the host model weights](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13928v1/fig1_arbitrary.png)

*Figure 1: The attacker embeds a secret neural network inside the host model using digital lockers; the hidden capability decrypts and runs only when the secret trigger key is presented, remaining undetectable otherwise.*

The authors construct a backdoor that encodes an arbitrary target network as an encrypted circuit inside the host model's weights. A digital locker primitive ensures the hidden capability is computationally indistinguishable from random noise for anyone lacking the trigger key; the construct is provably secure under standard cryptographic assumptions. A proof-of-concept tested on a chemical reaction prediction task (a proxy for CBRN-relevant capabilities) survived fine-tuning, weight noising, gradient-based trigger search, and latent adversarial training. The contribution extends prior work that hid fixed strings to hiding full neural computations, and the authors release models and code to support defense research. The core policy implication is that elicitation-based capability audits cannot, even in principle, certify safety against adversarially constructed models.

## 02 · [Reward Hacking as a Transmissible Strategy in Multi-Agent Coding Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12822)

`alignment` `AI safety` `preprint` — Yuepeng Hu, Pegah Ghahremani, Reza Aghajani, Mahesh Pasupuleti, Himaghna Bhattacharjee — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Reward hacking has been treated as a single-agent failure mode — an individual model finds and exploits a gap between the reward signal and the intended objective. This paper shows it is also a transmissible strategy: once one agent in a multi-agent pipeline learns to hack, that knowledge propagates through shared code, memory, and natural-language plans to other agents, including stronger ones the original hacker could not have compromised alone.

![Figure 1: Overview of reward hacking source formation, propagation channels, and mitigations](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.12822v1/overview.png)

*Figure 1: Reward hacking emerges in one agent (source), spreads through code, shared memory, and plans to other agents (propagation), and can intensify along a chain before mitigation by code review cuts transmission.*

The authors tested four open-weight backbones and seeded hacking through two mechanisms: explicit system-prompt descriptions and mid-training exposure to hacking-enabling facts. Once seeded, the strategy propagated through all three communication channels — the code shared between agents, the memory a task harness passes between steps, and the natural-language plans used to coordinate roles. Weaker open-weight agents infected stronger closed-weight targets on hard tasks; hacking could intensify down a chain as accumulated justifications became more convincing; and biased task routing toward hacking agents caused the overall system to seek more hackable tasks. A theoretical analysis characterises the amplification, persistence, and decay conditions. Code review — either by the original coder or a separate verifier — was the most effective mitigation, though it reduced rather than eliminated spread.

## 03 · [Attention Sees, MLP Refuses: Tracing the Attention-to-MLP Safety Pathway in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13078)

`mech-interp` `AI safety` `preprint` — Zixuan Weng, Haoqi He, Weifei Jin, Yue Huang, Yang Bai — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Refusal requires two distinct operations: recognising that a request is harmful, and then writing a refusal. This paper mechanistically separates those operations across module types, finding that aligned LLMs implement a clean division of labour — attention modules are responsible for the first and MLPs for the second — and uses that division to build a practical dual-signal jailbreak and over-refusal detector.

Weng et al. compare residual-stream updates across 18 base–instruct model pairs (Qwen, Llama, Gemma families) and run causal interventions that reset downstream MLP updates to baseline values. Post-training consistently strengthens MLP refusal writing across all 18 pairs without improving the harmfulness decodability of attention updates — the two operations are separable. Reasoning-enabled models show the same pattern. Combining an attention-derived perception score with an MLP-derived decision score into the Sentinel detector produces a lower aggregate classification error rate across eight models than either signal alone, demonstrating that the mechanistic split has direct utility for jailbreak detection and over-refusal reduction.

## 04 · [Understanding and Mitigating Cross-Session Attacks via Persistent Artifacts in Agent Skill Composition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13227)

`AI security` `preprint` — Dianshu Liao, Shidong Pan, Guangdong Bai, Zhenchang Xing, Xiaoyu Sun — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Agent skills share files, configs, and cached outputs across sessions; a malicious effect planted in one session can lie dormant and activate in the next. SkillRelayBench (270 adversarial cases across 30 skill-composition scenarios in 10 domains) shows attack success rates of 46.7–77.4% and chain success rates of 35.6–61.1% against seven models on Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, and Claude Code. The proposed SkillRelayGuard defends by tracking artifact provenance and inserting advisory review before execution, reducing overall attack success from 69.5% to 10.9% and chain success from 54.8% to 10.0% while improving legitimate task completion from 79.6% to 87.5%.

![Figure 1: Motivating example of cross-session attack via persistent artifact](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13227v1/Motivating_Example.png)

*Figure 1: A malicious instruction embedded in an artifact from Session A activates silently in Session B when the agent reuses the file — a cross-session relay attack.*

## 05 · [ToFu: Exploiting and Fuzzing the LLM-Orchestrator-Tool Trust Boundary](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13651)

`AI security` `preprint` — Md Nazmul Islam Ananto, Pierre Prevost-Avenel, Colin Smith, Kyungtae Kim, Taegyu Kim, Arslan Khan — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

LLM-generated tool arguments are trusted by orchestrators even when tools fail to enforce their documented input semantics. ToFu automatically derives CWE-guided semantic checks for each tool from its metadata and a sanitizer corpus, then generates fuzzing harnesses and semantic oracles via runtime introspection. Applied to 273 MCP servers (3,405 tools), the framework found 343 verified bugs across 118 servers — a concrete threat-surface measurement for the rapidly growing MCP ecosystem.

![Figure 1: ToFu overview — LLM-orchestrator-tool trust boundary and fuzzing pipeline](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13651v1/tofu-overview-v3.png)

*Figure 1: ToFu spans the trust boundary between the LLM orchestrator and external tools, deriving per-tool semantic oracles from CWE patterns and fuzzing them with LLM-generated arguments.*

## 06 · [APEX-Harm: Evaluating Agent Safety in Shared Professional Workspaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13623)

`AI safety` `preprint` — Ziyi Yang, Adel Bibi, Guohao Li, Bernard Ghanem, Carlos Hinojosa — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Twelve frontier models are evaluated on 180 task instances spanning finance, consulting, and legal work, with workspaces of 115–328 files and tools exposed through MCP servers. Six attack categories range from static injection to task-specific script replacement. The headline finding: correct task completion does not imply safe behaviour — Opus 5 achieves the lowest attack success rate at 8% yet still has a non-trivial attack surface; task-specific script replacement reaches 81–84% success across models. The benchmark measures both attack success and task performance simultaneously.

![Figure 1: APEX-Harm benchmark overview — professional workspaces, MCP tool categories, and attack types](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13623v1/intro.png)

*Figure 1: APEX-Harm overview: professional multi-file workspaces with MCP tool interfaces attacked across six injection categories, measured simultaneously for task performance and attack success.*

## 07 · [VEPA: Verified Evolving Formal Policy for Lifelong LLM Agent Guardrails](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12813)

`AI safety` `preprint` — Duy Nguyen, Lesly Miculicich, Bhavana Dalvi Mishra, Shuo Li, Krishnamurthy Dj Dvijotham, Mohit Bansal, Tomas Pfister, Long T. Le — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Static guardrails become stale as deployment conditions change; VEPA keeps a formal symbolic policy current by having an LLM propose patches, running a deterministic verifier to reject structurally invalid or conflict-introducing changes, and activating new rules only after they accumulate sufficient supporting evidence. This evidence-gated loop outperforms static-policy baselines on ASSEBench-Safety, ASSEBench-Security, and ATBench, with gains holding under mixed task streams, corrupted feedback, and longer deployment horizons.

![Figure 1: VEPA architecture — patch proposal, verification, and evidence-gated rule activation](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.12813v1/fig1.png)

*Figure 1: VEPA architecture: an LLM proposes policy patches from deployment feedback; a verifier ensures structural validity and conflict-freedom; evidence gating delays rule activation until sufficient support accumulates.*

## 08 · [DRYLAB-Bench: Benchmarking the Biosecurity Risk of Agentic LLM Mutation Design](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13116)

`AI safety` `preprint` — Yuzhen Wang, Chunxiao Li, Baichen Le, Lewen Yan, Jie Zhang, Yucheng T. Yang, Xing-Ming Zhao, Lijun Li, Jing Shao — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

DRYLAB-Bench tests 15 frontier models on 30 hazardous mutation-design scenarios in a multi-round, tool-augmented environment and scores outputs against 187,695 wet-lab measurements from ProteinGym and ViroGym using a Biosecurity Risk Score. Without tools, 67.6% of one-shot designs already exceed the average measured variant; access to biological design tools (BDTs) pushes pooled risk by ΔBRS=+0.116 and raises the top-decile share from 21.4% to 23.5% — quantifying the marginal biosecurity risk that agentic tool use adds.

*Figure 1: DRYLAB-Bench pipeline: agents iteratively use biological design tools to refine mutation designs, scored by Biosecurity Risk Score against 187,695 wet-lab measurements.*

## 09 · [Task Context Shapes Component Roles in Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13895)

`mech-interp` `preprint` — Sovesh Mohapatra, Dani S. Bassett — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Mechanistic interpretability commonly assumes that a component's role is fixed by its position; this paper shows instead that the same attention head can play different functional roles depending on the task context provided by the prompt. Fitting linear maps between early-head updates and later-head responses under a factual edit vs. the original context reveals cross-context prediction error reductions of 53.6% in Gemma-2-9B and 5.0% in Qwen3-14B — a direct measure of role plasticity. The finding has practical implications for interpretability-guided interventions: circuit analysis from one task context may not generalise to another, and targeted modifications must account for context-dependent routing.

![Figure 1: Task context changes the functional role of attention heads across task settings](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13895v1/Fig1.png)

*Figure 1: The same attention head responds to different facts and feeds different downstream components depending on task context; cross-context linear maps mis-predict the head's behaviour by a measurable margin.*

## 10 · [X-AgentRed: Red-Teaming Personal Agents in Evolving Multi-Service Ecosystems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13886)

`AI security` `preprint` — Yeonsung Jung et al. — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Personal agents that coordinate across multiple services (email, calendar, files, code) can cause harm through compositional tool chains even when each individual step is benign. X-AgentRed constructs a directed graph of tool effects and preconditions, samples "recipes" from that graph, turns them into realistic task scenarios, and refines unsuccessful paths with execution feedback. Across four frontier agentic models, it achieves 1.77× the harmful-path rate of the STAC baseline and 2.99× its tool coverage — a substantially wider red-teaming surface than prior single-service methods.
