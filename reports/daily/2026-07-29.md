## 01 · [Breaking Bad: Interpretability-Based Safety Audits of State-of-the-Art LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20945)

`mech-interp` `AI security` `peer-reviewed` `NeurIPS 2025`

Interpretability tools were built to understand models, not to attack them. Breaking Bad shows that the same directional geometry that makes activation steering explanatory also makes it a potent red-teaming instrument — and that widely-deployed frontier models disagree radically in their vulnerability, with Llama-3.3-70B-4bt fully compromised and GPT-oss-120B essentially immune.

![Figure 1: Jailbreak ASR across 8 LLMs via Universal Steering and Representation Engineering](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.20945/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Universal Steering achieves 91% jailbreak rate on Llama-3.3-70B-4bt and 83% on the same model via RepE, while GPT-oss-120B remains robust to both interpretability-based attacks across all tested unsafe concepts.*

Eight production-grade open-weight LLMs (Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.3-70B-4bt, GPT-oss-20B, GPT-oss-120B, Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-32B, Phi4-3.8B, Phi4-14B) are audited via Universal Steering (US) and Representation Engineering (RepE) — both of which add a learned directional vector to the residual stream at inference time. An adaptive two-stage grid search identifies optimal coefficient magnitudes for each unsafe concept. The failure mode of the Llama family is striking: Llama-3.3-70B-4bt reaches 91% ASR under US and 83% under RepE without any access to the model's internals beyond its weight matrices. The authors attribute the divergence between Llama (vulnerable) and GPT-oss (robust) to alignment geometry — robust models distribute safety representations diffusely across layers, making a single-direction intervention insufficient. Published NeurIPS 2025; this is the strongest empirical evidence to date that activation-space interpretability methods are dual-use safety audit instruments.

## 02 · [Position: Mechanistic Interpretability Should Prioritize Feature Consistency in SAEs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20254)

`mech-interp` `peer-reviewed` `ACL 2026`

Sparse autoencoders are valuable only if they reliably recover the same features across independent training runs. This ACL 2026 position paper argues that feature consistency — not reconstruction loss or interpretability scores — should be the primary optimization target, introduces the PW-MCC metric to measure it, and shows that TopK SAEs can already achieve PW-MCC = 0.80 on LLM activations with the right architectural choices.

![Figure 1: PW-MCC feature consistency across SAE families and training seeds — TopK achieves highest cross-run convergence](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20254/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Pairwise Dictionary Mean Correlation Coefficient (PW-MCC) across five SAE architectures and three training seeds on LLM activation data. TopK SAEs achieve PW-MCC = 0.80; standard ReLU SAEs reach only 0.54, meaning ~46% of features change substantially across runs.*

Authors: Xiangchen Song, Aashiq Muhamed, Yujia Zheng, Lingjing Kong, Zeyu Tang, Mona T. Diab, Virginia Smith, Kun Zhang. The core argument: if two independent SAE training runs on the same model activations produce different feature dictionaries, the causal claims built on either run are not about the model — they are about the training run. The PW-MCC metric measures this by computing the average maximum correlation between each feature in dictionary A and any feature in dictionary B, providing a single scalar summary of cross-run convergence. The paper demonstrates that TopK SAEs converge significantly better than ReLU/Gated/JumpReLU variants on Gemma-2-9B activations, and that this advantage cannot be explained by reconstruction loss differences alone. ACL 2026 long paper track. Code at [github.com/xiangchensong/sae-feature-consistency](https://github.com/xiangchensong/sae-feature-consistency).

## 03 · [Building Fast, Evaluating Slow: Pipeline Choices Dominate Autointerpretability Score Variance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.19386)

`mech-interp` `preprint` — ETH Zürich — arXiv, July 2026

Cross-paper SAE comparisons often hinge on autointerpretability scores — an LLM explains a feature, another scores the explanation, and the number travels into leaderboards and ablation tables. This paper conducts the systematic audit those comparisons have needed: autointerpretability score variance is dominated by pipeline configuration, not SAE architecture.

![Figure 1: Variance decomposition — pipeline choices vs architecture across four metrics on Pythia-160M and Apertus-8B](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19386/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Methodological variance (corpus sampling, draw conditions, evaluator model, prompt template) exceeds architectural variance across all four autointerpretability metrics on both tested models. The detection metric is most stable; fuzzing is unreliable across all conditions.*

Systematic experiments span four autointerpretability metrics (simulation, detection, fuzzing, purity), two models (Pythia-160M and Apertus-8B), and four axes of methodological variation. Three results emerge. R1: across all metrics and both models, methodological variance collectively exceeds the variance explained by SAE architecture. R2: each metric has a distinct instability profile — detection is the most stable, fuzzing essentially unreliable across conditions, purity sensitive to corpus composition. R3: top-k feature rankings do not stay consistent across corpus and draw conditions, meaning aggregate scores paper over per-feature variance that matters for mechanistic claims. Implication: existing published comparisons of SAE architectures based on autointerpretability scores may reflect pipeline differences rather than differences in feature quality, and any new paper in this space needs an ablation over pipeline variables to isolate the architectural signal.

**04 · [Agent Data Injection Attacks are Realistic Threats to AI Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05120)**
`AI security` — arXiv, July 6, 2026

![ADI attack flow: malicious data disguised as trusted resource identifier or context metadata → agent interprets as authorized instruction](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05120/x1.png)

*Figure 4: ADI attack chain — attacker-controlled data is embedded in metadata fields agents treat as authoritative (resource identifiers, data origin tags, context keys), bypassing standard IPI filters that check only free-form text content.*

Indirect prompt injection (IPI) attacks embed adversarial instructions in content an agent retrieves. ADI (Agent Data Injection) is a distinct category: the malicious payload is disguised as trusted structural metadata rather than free-form text — resource identifiers, data origin fields, context keys — that agents process as authoritative rather than as content to be filtered. Because standard IPI defenses check text fields, ADI bypasses them entirely. The paper shows ADI is practical across multiple deployed agent frameworks and requires only attacker control of metadata fields, not text content.

**06 · [Bifocal Diffusion Language Models: Asymmetric Bidirectional Context for Parallel Generation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27732)**
`dLLM` — Yuhang Chen, Xianfeng Wu, Jinhao Duan et al. (Meta AI / UNC Chapel Hill) — arXiv, June 26, 2026

![R2LM architecture: causal attention head (left context, KV-cache compatible) + Mamba state-space layer (right context, position-aware)](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.27732/x1.png)

*Figure 6: Bifocal dLLM architecture resolves the KV-cache incompatibility of bidirectional attention by using causal attention for left context (cache-compatible) and Mamba for continuous right context, enabling serving throughput competitive with AR models while preserving full-context generation quality.*

Full bidirectional attention in masked dLLMs gives high generation quality but requires recomputing attention over the full sequence at every denoising step, blocking KV caching and degrading serving throughput. Bifocal dLLMs (instantiated as R2LM) use standard causal attention for left context — preserving KV cache compatibility — and a Mamba state-space layer for right context, delivering continuous position-aware right-side information without the quadratic recomputation cost. The asymmetric design matches bidirectional-attention dLLM quality on standard generation benchmarks while closing most of the serving throughput gap with AR models.

**07 · [Accelerating Masked Diffusion Large Language Models: A Survey of Efficient Inference Techniques](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12829)**
`dLLM` — Daehoon Gwak, Minhyung Lee, Junwoo Park, Jaegul Choo — arXiv, July 14, 2026

![Taxonomy of masked dLLM inference acceleration: three branches — Algorithm (remasking, confidence, block), Architecture (caching, speculative), System (batching, hardware)](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12829/x1.png)

*Figure 7: Taxonomy of inference acceleration techniques for masked dLLMs, organized across algorithm-level (remasking strategies, confidence-based commit, block diffusion), architecture-level (diffusion-aware caching, speculative decoding), and system-level (heterogeneous batching, hardware mapping) axes.*

First comprehensive survey of inference acceleration methods specifically tailored to masked dLLMs. Covers three axes: algorithm-level (confidence remasking, block diffusion, trajectory-aware commit gating), architecture-level (diffusion-aware KV-cache reuse, speculative diffusion), and system-level (heterogeneous request batching, CUDA kernel design for parallel decoding). The survey also benchmarks available open-source dLLMs (LLaDA, Dream, MDLM, iLLaDA) and situates each acceleration technique against the model's specific bottleneck — a useful guide for practitioners targeting latency vs throughput vs quality tradeoffs in production dLLM deployment.

**08 · [Size Doesn't Matter: Cosine-Scored Sparse Autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15054)**
`mech-interp` — Silen Naihin, Lev Stambler — arXiv, June 2026

![Cosine vs inner-product SAE: inner product allocates features to norm detectors; cosine SAE allocates all features to content-aligned concepts](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15054/x1.png)

*Figure 8: Standard inner-product SAE activations scale with both directional alignment and input norm, causing a portion of the dictionary to detect token norms (after sublayer normalization has removed magnitude from the model's residual stream). Cosine-scored SAEs train an optimizer-chosen blend of cosine similarity and magnitude; training always recovers toward pure cosine, filling those wasted slots with content-aligned features.*

Standard SAE activation scores depend on both feature direction and input norm — but sublayer normalization in transformers discards magnitude before the residual stream, so a norm-sensitive score detects a quantity the model does not read. Naihin and Stambler replace the inner-product encoder with a learned blend of cosine similarity and input magnitude; in all experiments, training optimizes to near-zero magnitude dependence, confirming that norm sensitivity is a score-geometry artifact rather than a useful signal. At matched reconstruction loss, cosine encoders learn features that align with human-recognizable semantic concepts measurably more often, filling the dictionary slots that inner-product encoders waste on norm detectors. The advantage is not universal across tasks or depths, but the authors argue cosine scoring should be the default for dictionary learning on layer-normalized representations.

**09 · [Can Language Model Agents be Helpful Circuit Explainers in Mechanistic Interpretability?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.24026)**
`mech-interp` — Anthropic — arXiv, June 2026

![Two-loop multiagent mech-interp framework: outer explanation refinement loop + inner feature discovery loop, both driven by LLM agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.24026/x1.png)

*Figure 9: Two coupled agent loops: the outer loop refines and validates explanations for known circuits using contrastive activation analysis; the inner loop discovers new features in the model that the explanation loop's failures surface as unexplained residuals. Together they automate the hand-authored circuit-explanation workflow.*

Anthropic paper asking whether LLM agents can automate the circuit-explanation workflow that has so far required expert human interpretability researchers. The framework runs two coupled loops: an outer explanation-refinement loop where an agent proposes, tests, and revises natural-language hypotheses about circuit behavior using contrastive activation experiments; and an inner feature-discovery loop that targets hypotheses the outer loop cannot confirm — surfacing new SAE features as unexplained residuals. Evaluated on canonical circuits in Gemma-family models; the agent framework correctly explains the majority of manually-authored circuit elements and discovers several features not in the original manual analysis.

**10 · [Sangam: Efficiently Serving Diffusion LLMs with the AR Stack](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04206)**
`dLLM` — arXiv, July 4, 2026

![Sangam colocated serving: 9–20% mean latency reduction vs hybrid AR-dLLM execution across prefill-heavy and decode-heavy workloads](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04206/x1.png)

*Figure 10: Colocated serving of dLLMs within the AR inference stack reduces mean latency by 9–20% over hybrid execution on most workloads; hybrid execution retains an advantage only for extreme prefill-heavy workloads where the AR prefix vastly exceeds the dLLM generation segment.*

dLLMs commit multiple token positions per forward pass, which their inference stacks historically handle by alternating between an AR-serving backend (for context ingestion) and a separate dLLM denoising backend. Sangam unifies both within the AR serving stack: dLLM denoising steps are expressed as batched AR passes over masked positions, enabling colocated execution that shares KV-cache infrastructure, GPU kernels, and batching logic. The result is 9–20% mean latency reduction over the hybrid-execution baseline on mixed workloads. The framework is practical for teams already operating AR inference infrastructure who want to add dLLM capabilities without a separate stack.
