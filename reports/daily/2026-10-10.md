## 01 · [Is Memorization Context-Sensitive? Prefix-Based Extraction Beyond Isolated Prefixes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12085)

`AI security` `EMNLP 2026` — Ali Satvaty, Narjes Sharafi, Jirui Qi, Suzan Verberne, Fatih Turkmen — EMNLP 2026 (accepted, peer-reviewed)

The standard assumption that retrieval-augmented context suppresses memorization extraction turns out to be wrong in a subtle but important way. Context doesn't eliminate memorization—it reshapes the extractable set, sometimes enabling extraction of samples that isolated-prefix testing would never catch.

![Figure 2: Jaccard overlap heatmap of extractable samples under different context conditions](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.12085v1/28_jaccard_overlap_heatmap.png)

*Figure 2: Jaccard overlap between sets of extractable samples under empty-prompt vs. RAG-context conditions, revealing a "robust core" that persists across all contexts and a "sensitive boundary" that context shifts.*

The authors score suffix log-probability under empty prompts and under retrieved contexts of varying relevance across three open-weight instruction-tuned models. They find that extractable memorization has a "context-robust core and a context-sensitive boundary": samples that are extractable without context remain extractable in most contextual settings (especially as prefix length grows), while context primarily suppresses or enables samples near the extraction threshold—meaning context-enabled extractability is a distinct security risk missed by isolated-prefix evaluation. Accepted at EMNLP 2026.

## 02 · [RouterInterp: Understanding Superposed Specialisation in Mixture of Experts Routing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11775)

`mech-interp` `preprint` — Ilya Lasy, Nora Yinuo Cai, Kola Ayonrinde — arXiv preprint (cs.LG, 8 Oct 2026)

MoE experts don't specialise in a single broad domain—they specialise in a disjoint union of fine-grained features, which is why token-statistics-based interpretability of routing has consistently failed. RouterInterp uses SAE latents to make routing decisions readable for the first time.

![Figure 3: RouterInterp pipeline—gradient-based SAE latent attribution → grouping by expert → natural-language explanation](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11775v1/assets/main_figure.png)

*Figure 3: RouterInterp identifies the top-45 SAE latents predictive of each expert's routing decision via gradient attribution, groups them, then generates a unified natural-language explanation of when that expert is activated.*

The Superposed Specialisation Hypothesis (SSH) holds that each expert routes on a union of unrelated fine-grained features rather than one coherent domain; RouterInterp tests this by selecting the top-45 SAE latents per expert via gradient-based attribution, collecting positive/negative activation windows, and generating prose explanations with an LLM synthesiser. On gpt-oss-20b, RouterInterp achieves ~0.49 F1 vs ~0.38 for Expert Impact AutoInterp and ~0.30 for Unigram Lookup; on OLMoE-1B-7B, ~0.60 F1 vs ~0.31–0.34 baselines. Per-expert analysis confirms SSH: each expert's top-20 latents cluster into ~11 distinct semantic groups on average. Ablations show the gain comes from SAE feature grouping, not the LLM explainer.

## 03 · [Not Every Change Is Necessary: Recoverable Drift in Large Language Model Unlearning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11915)

`alignment` `mech-interp` `preprint` — Xunlei Chen, Qinghui Gong, Jingkun Xue, Qihe Liu, Shijie Zhou, Fei Ye — arXiv preprint (cs.CL, 8 Oct 2026)

Current unlearning methods overshoot: the parameter changes that achieve target forgetting collaterally damage unrelated capabilities, but much of that damage can be reversed post-hoc without re-introducing the forgotten knowledge. PTP-U formalises this and sets a new Pareto frontier on the forgetting–retention trade-off.

![Figure 1: Motivating illustration of recoverable vs. necessary drift in PTP-U unlearning](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11915v1/Intro.png)

*Figure 1: Unlearning edits create two kinds of parameter changes—those necessary for forgetting and those that are recoverable "drift" on non-target capabilities. PTP-U targets the former and reverses the latter.*

Propose-Then-Project Unlearning (PTP-U) has two stages. Stage I applies closed-form K-FAC-curvature-guided analytic edits in a low-dimensional subspace, updating Attention then FFN with sequential re-linearisation between them, to maximally concentrate forgetting at the relevant parameters. Stage II projects back toward the original model's output distributions on non-target data under a nonlinear policy that keeps forgetting constraints satisfied. On MUSE-Books (Llama2-7B), PTP-U achieves BLEU 8.61 and ROUGE-L 6.33 with MMLU 43.34—the best combined forgetting and utility among 8 baselines (NPO_KL, RMU, WHP, ALTER, ASU, ICUL, SCANS, MET). On RWKU (Llama3.1-8B-Instruct), it attains 94.20% average non-target utility with the lowest AA score (15.20).

## 04 · [Safe Actions Alone Do Not Ensure Safe Agents: Identifying Unfulfilled Obligations with Guard Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11773)

`AI security` `preprint` — Youwei Feng, Yitong Zhang, Yuetong Liu, Jia Li — arXiv preprint (cs.AI, 8 Oct 2026)

Guard models focused on forbidden actions miss the majority of agent safety failures. In a preliminary study on SusVibes, 56.92% of GLM-5.3 trajectories contained unfulfilled obligations vs. only 30.00% containing forbidden actions.

*Figure 1: Two guard-model tasks—forbidden action identification (Task 1) and obligation identification (Task 2)—illustrated on example agent trajectories.*

ObligationBench (240 expert-validated trajectories, 3 coding task types) tests whether guard models can catch required-but-missing actions. ObligationGuard, a Qwen3-8B fine-tuned on 40K GPT-5.6-Sol-generated synthetic trajectories, reaches 57.52% recall / 21.67% exact match, versus 48.97% recall / 10.00% exact match for the best evaluated off-the-shelf model. Using ObligationGuard as feedback raises a Qwen3.8-27B coding agent's secure pass rate from 6.5% to 15.1% while barely affecting functional pass rate (27.4% → 26.9%); Spearman's ρ = 0.94 between ObligationBench recall and secure-pass improvement across 6 guard models.

## 05 · [Same Outcome, Different Evidence: Intent Recovery in LLM Safety Evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11766)

`AI security` `preprint` — Haitong Jiang, Chunlin Liu, Sihan Tang, Chan Wu, Xiaoqing Su, Yuhong Feng — arXiv preprint (cs.CL, 8 Oct 2026)

Attack success rate alone obscures whether a model ever engaged with the harmful request. For GPT-4o, Toki Pona and CaesarCipher both yield 2.9% ASR—but operative understanding rates (UR) are 15.2% vs. 96.2%, requiring very different interpretations.

![Figure 1: IRIS framework diagram — ASR alongside operative understanding rate (UR)](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11766v1/iris_attribution.png)

*Figure 1: IRIS annotates responses along two independent axes—intent recognition and harmful assistance—exposing cases where low ASR reflects non-engagement rather than safety alignment.*

IRIS (Intent Recovery as an Intermediate Signal) adds UR to safety evaluation: it measures whether a response both recognises the harmful task and treats it as the task to answer. Testing on 105 harmful tasks from HarmBench/StrongREJECT/JailbreakBench, across 6 models with an automated judge (mistral-large-2512) checked by human raters, IRIS shows that UR and ASR track different phenomena. Three increasingly explicit English reconstructions of the same compressed prompts raise UR by 15.2–41.0 pp for all 6 models while ASR shows no consistent direction—evidence that "safety" via low ASR can be an artifact of obfuscation opacity rather than genuine alignment.

## 06 · [Safe at One Loop, Risky at Another: Aligning Safety Across Recurrent Depths in Looped Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10625)

`AI security` `preprint` — Yi Wang, Xiuyuan Qi, Dongqi Han, Dongsheng Li, Wenjie Wang — arXiv preprint (cs.LG, 8 Oct 2026)

Looped LMs share weights across recurrent steps, but a single depth-1 alignment pass leaves deeper depths highly exploitable—vanilla Ouro-1.4B reaches 61.79% ASR at d=3 vs. 29.91% at d=1. SafeBridge reduces overall ASR from 21.06% (DPO) to 4.56%.

![Figure 1: SafeBridge introduction — jailbreak ASR by inference depth in looped language models](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10625v1/SafeBridge_intro.png)

*Figure 1: Jailbreak attack success rate (ASR) grows with inference depth in vanilla and DPO-aligned Ouro models; SafeBridge keeps it uniformly low by combining depth-specific FiLM modulation, selective state bridging, and cross-depth safety supervision.*

SafeBridge adds ~0.02% parameters (299K for 1.4B) via: depth-specific FiLM scale/shift at layers d=2–4; a gated connection that bridges the hidden state from depth 2 into later depths; and safety-signed cross-depth supervision with a penalty for depth-to-depth ASR drops. On Ouro-1.4B, it reduces combined attack ASR from 21.06% (DPO) and 20.85% (SafeDPO) to 4.56%; on 2.6B from 44.93%/44.30% to 9.85%. Cross-depth transfer attacks (built at d=2, deployed at d=3) drop from 66.24% to 12.02% on 2.6B; PAIR-style pooled attacks drop from 85.49% to 24.25%.

## 07 · [OnTrack: Real-Time Monitoring and Intervention in LLM Agent Trajectories via Streaming Structure-Aware Optimal Transport](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12375)

`AI security` `preprint` — Babak Barazandeh, Connor Swanson, Chinmay Kulkarni, Nikhil Mungel — arXiv preprint (cs.AI, 8 Oct 2026)

Per-step agent monitoring in ~1ms via streaming optimal transport over dependency graphs; using only the first 8 steps, it ranks failing SWE-bench trajectories below succeeding ones with +0.057 AUROC over baselines, and an abort policy saves 18% of compute on failing runs.

*Figure 1: OnTrack system diagram—per-step dependency graph compared against reference successful runs via streaming structure-aware OT, with alert/abort outputs.*

OnTrack models each agent trajectory as a growing dependency graph (nodes = steps, edges = artifact consumption), then compares it against recorded successful runs using an OT objective combining linear matching cost (cosine distance on action text/args, tool-substitution cost), a quadratic structure penalty on dependency-hop mismatches, KL penalties for unmatched mass, and entropic regularisation (α=0.45, β=0.20, δ=0.35, θ=0.35, λ=0.3). A streaming warm-started conditional-gradient solver runs in ~1ms/step. On SWE-bench, early-prefix ranking achieves +0.057 AUROC; an abort policy saves 18% of compute on failing runs, with 83% of aborted runs confirmed to have been heading to failure (5 of 6 aborts correct).

## 08 · [Searching for "Harmful Refusal": A Psychometric Audit of an AI Safety Benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12409)

`AI security` `COLM 2026` — Christopher M. Stewart, Preston Botter, Natalie Sarabosing, Muye Zhang, Rachel Phinnemore, Shalini Ghosh, Hong Shen, Hoda Heidari — COLM 2026 AIMS Workshop (peer-reviewed)

HarmBench does not measure a single "harmful refusal" construct: a 3D IRT model fits substantially better than 1D (held-out log-loss 0.258 vs. 0.470), and 13 DIF items between OpenAI and Anthropic models collapse to 1–2 under 3D scoring.

![Figure 2: Bernoulli-null eigenvalue check of HarmBench item-correlation matrix](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.12409v1/harmbench_item_correlation_parallel_analysis_nozoom.png)

*Figure 2: Parallel analysis of HarmBench item correlations showing multiple eigenvalues above the random-data cutoff, arguing against a single underlying dimension.*

Using a construct validity framework, the authors screen HELM Safety's four candidate datasets and find three saturated (pass rate 0.92–0.94); they then apply exploratory 2PL MIRT (1–10 dimensions) and confirmatory 3D (standard/contextual/copyright) and 7D (HarmBench harm-domain) models to HarmBench, compared by held-out log-loss across five 80/20 splits. The 3D model (log-loss 0.258) beats the best unidimensional 3PL (0.322) in all five splits; removing copyright items, a 2D model still beats 1D (0.278 vs. 0.293). DIF flags 13 OpenAI-vs-Anthropic items under aggregate scoring, which collapse to 1–2 under 3D scope-specific matching. The implication: aggregate safety benchmarks can conflate distinct harm behaviors and inflate apparent developer differences.

## 09 · [Looking Inside LLMs: Small-World Connectivity as a Signature of Reasoning Performance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12304)

`mech-interp` `preprint` — Zheng Huang, Sansheng Cao, Enpei Zhang, Weikang Qiu, Elynn Chen, Xiang Zhang, Yaoqing Yang, Rex Ying, Dawei Zhou, Yujun Yan — arXiv preprint (cs.LG, 8 Oct 2026)

Attention heads in stronger LLMs form tighter small-world graphs (high local clustering, short global paths); SWA pruning guided by per-head core/bridge scores reduces WikiText perplexity from 177.80 to 118.67 at 0.7 sparsity with Wanda on Llama 3.2-3B.

![Figure 1: Pipeline diagram — attention-head functional graphs from activation similarity → SWI → SWA pruning](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.12304v1/nips_figure_26_new.png)

*Figure 1: Functional graphs built from pairwise attention-head activation similarities; the small-world index (SWI) computed from them correlates with reasoning performance across models and training checkpoints.*

The authors compute pairwise activation similarity across attention heads to build functional graphs, then measure the small-world index (SWI = local clustering / characteristic path length, normalized to a random baseline). SWI tracks fluid reasoning across 6 LLMs (Qwen3-14B SWI 1.82, NLL 0.082; Llama-3.2-1B SWI 0.92, NLL 0.169) and rises monotonically with training in Pythia-6.9B (SWI 0.46 → 1.19 over 8B–300B tokens). Core and bridge head scores guide Small-World Allocation (SWA) sparsity; at 0.7 sparsity with Wanda, SWA lowers perplexity from 177.80 to 118.67 vs. 125.1 for the best alternative (ATP).

## 10 · [A Security Meta-Model for Retrieval-Augmented Generation Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11893)

`AI security` `preprint` — Steve Nouyep, Sébastien Salva, Maxime Puys — arXiv preprint (cs.CR, 8 Oct 2026)

A structured causal framework synthesising 43 RAG-security publications into a filterable catalog of 38 attacks, 41 risks, and 72 mitigations—with an interactive web visualizer. Key finding: 42% of attacks target document ingestion, and 72% of the corpus is attack-focused vs. 21% defense.

![Figure 1: Web visualizer rendering the security catalog as a causal chain from RAG surface through attack → weakness → risk → CIA impact](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11893v1/figures/v1_sdoc_cwe345_webapp.png)

*Figure 1: The interactive catalog visualiser traces a causal chain from a specific RAG surface (e.g., document ingestion) through attack, weakness, and risk to CIA impact, filterable by deployment properties.*

The meta-model defines 7 entity types (RAG, Surface, Attack, Weakness, Risk, Mitigation, CIA) with structural constraints requiring every Attack to be enabled by a Surface, exploit a Weakness, and generate a Risk. Filtering by 6 deployment properties (type, ingestion source, network exposure, memory, LLM location, agentic) yields a deployment-specific risk profile; in a worst-case multimodal/public/cloud configuration, 40 of 41 risks remain applicable. The catalog identifies 5 unmitigated risks (R17, R33–R36), an output-integrity coverage gap (25% gap ratio for OUTPUT surface), 16 poisoning attacks concentrated at document ingestion, and full or partial OWASP LLM Top 10 coverage for 9 of 10 entries.
