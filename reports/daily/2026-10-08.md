### 1. [**Component and Dimension Sparsity in Transformer Refusal Mechanisms**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06903)
- **Authors / venue:** Vincent Siu, Glenn Grant-Richards, Vlad Pavlovich, Yizhou Sun, Dawn Song, Chenguang Wang — **COLM 2026 (accepted)**
- **Why it ranks here:** The only peer-reviewed entry today and the most actionable piece of mechanistic interpretability this cycle: it identifies the sparse subset of components and residual-stream dimensions that carry refusal, opening a precise intervention target for both attacks and defenses.
- **Technical summary:** The authors apply activation steering across four open-weight models and perform both component-level and dimension-level ablation sweeps to identify the minimal mechanism behind refusal. They find that 28–48% of upstream components account for 88–101% of the full steering effect; within those components, roughly half of the residual-stream dimensions retain 85–98% of that component-level baseline. The two-level sparsity result is consistent with a privileged-basis structure and implies that current refusal-removal attacks likely hit non-causal dimensions, explaining their fragility.

![Figure 1: Two-level sparsity in transformer refusal mechanisms — component sparsity map and dimension concentration](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06903/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Component and dimension selection results showing that a sparse 28–48% of upstream components captures nearly the full refusal steering effect, with further concentration in ~50% of dimensions within those components.*

### 2. [**SafeEvo: Deciphering the Safety Alignment Mechanism and Evolution in Language Models**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09600)
- **Authors / venue:** Miao Yu, Hao Huang, Lu Yuan, Yunpeng Li, Kun Wang, Zuming Jiang — arXiv preprint (cs.AI, 7 Oct 2026)
- **Why it ranks here:** First paper to trace refusal circuits from the pretrained base through every alignment checkpoint, showing they are latent from the start—with direct implications for how safety survives fine-tuning attacks.
- **Technical summary:** SafeEvo frames circuit extraction as a differentiable mask-optimization problem over model units, isolating the sparse subgraph that carries full safety ability. On Llama-3-8B, Qwen-2.5-7B, and Mistral-7B, causally ablating the discovered "weak refusal circuit" in the base model eliminates refusal with attack success rates of 32.7–48.4%. Tracking circuits across 20 alignment checkpoints with warm-started extraction reveals that adjacent-checkpoint circuit overlap falls from 98% at alignment onset to 78% at checkpoint 20, and independently extracted circuits share only 26% overlap—evidence for circuit non-uniqueness. The proposed Safety Circuit Alignment (SCA) method confines safety gradient updates to the refusal circuit, mitigating the alignment tax.

![Figure 2: SafeEvo circuit evolution diagram showing overlap between adjacent alignment checkpoints](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.09600/x1.png)

*Figure 2: Refusal circuit overlap declining from 98% to 78% across 20 alignment checkpoints, illustrating that alignment continuously restructures the safety circuit rather than merely strengthening a fixed one.*

### 3. [**Backdooring Sparse Autoencoders**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06049)
- **Authors / venue:** Enrico Ahlers, Daniel Passon, Tobias Kiecker, Eik Reichmann, Lars Grunske — arXiv preprint (cs.CR, 5 Oct 2026)
- **Why it ranks here:** Opens a previously unrecognized attack surface: SAEs used as intervention layers can be silently trojanized, co-opting the interpretability toolchain as a malware delivery vector without touching the underlying LLM.
- **Technical summary:** The attack modifies only the SAE decoder weights while leaving both the LM and the SAE encoder intact, confining the backdoor to one auxiliary component at one insertion layer. In a code-generation case study across three models and a broad range of insertion layers, the modified SAE achieves high rates of unsolicited malicious code insertion with trigger-dependent behavior conditioned on a prompt cue. Critically, strong backdoor behavior co-exists with only small changes in conventional SAE quality scores (reconstruction loss, feature density), meaning standard SAEBench metrics would not flag the tampered component. The authors conclude that any SAE intended for in-context steering should be treated as a security-sensitive artifact.

![Figure 3: Supply-chain diagram showing backdoored SAE inserted between LLM layers](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06049/x1.png)

*Figure 3: Attack architecture — the SAE decoder is modified offline; at inference, the SAE processes activations and the poisoned reconstruction drives the LM to emit attacker-chosen content on trigger, while standard quality metrics remain nearly unchanged.*

### 4. [**APEX: Active Protection at Execution Boundaries for LLM Agents**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06966)

`AI security` `preprint` — Zheng et al. — arXiv (cs.CR, 3 Oct 2026)

Shifts indirect-prompt-injection defense from detecting attack patterns to verifying the agent's proposed action at execution time: APEX checks whether the action is authorized and whether the runtime information that reached it was endorsed by the original task. Evaluated against 13 baselines across six benchmarks, APEX achieves 0% attack success on five of six benchmarks and 0.56% on the sixth, while maintaining high utility.

![Figure 4: APEX execution-boundary verification architecture](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06966/x1.png)

*Figure 4: APEX wraps each agent action with a backward-and-forward contract check (WRAP) and a deception probe (PLANT), achieving near-zero ASR on five of six benchmarks.*

### 5. [**Does On-Policy Distillation for Safety Pose Backdoor Risks?**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07654)

`AI security` `alignment` `preprint` — Jian Luo et al. — arXiv (cs.CR, 6 Oct 2026)

Demonstrates that a safety-aligned teacher can silently transfer backdoor behavior to a student through on-policy distillation even when the student's pretraining was clean: at 3% data poisoning, attack success rate on the student reaches 70%, rising to 67% with just 10 poisoned samples after 16 training epochs. Top-k KL estimation accelerates backdoor transfer relative to sampled-token KL. The proposed "Lazy Defense" clips KL rewards to slow student updates and delays transfer at low poisoning rates.

![Figure 5: Backdoor transfer via on-policy distillation—teacher to student attack success rate vs poisoning rate](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.07654/x1.png)

*Figure 5: Attack success rate of the distilled student as a function of poisoning rate, showing that even ≤3% poisoning of the teacher's data yields ~70% ASR in the student.*

### 6. [**Prompted to Discriminate: Generalizing Malicious-Input Probes in the Wild**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02413)

`mech-interp` `AI security` `preprint` — Elad David, Max Fomin — arXiv (1 Oct 2026)

Appending a short classification suffix after the user turn at probe-read time improves out-of-distribution generalization of activation-based malicious-input detectors by up to ~4 AUC points under strict leave-one-dataset-out evaluation across 13 safety benchmarks (jailbreak, injection, benign) and three model families (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3.5-9B, Gemma-4-12B). Format matters more than content: a content-free classification suffix matches the real benign/malicious label suffix, while off-topic suffixes help little. Deployed via KV-cache fork, it is a near-zero-cost drop-in improvement for any activation-probe monitor.

![Figure 6: Probe AUC improvement from classification suffix across model families](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.02413/x1.png)

*Figure 6: Leave-one-dataset-out AUC for malicious-input probes with and without the classification suffix, showing consistent gain on Llama and Gemma across jailbreak and injection benchmarks.*

### 7. [**Secure Speculative Decoding for Large Language Models**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08678)

`AI security` `preprint` — Yichi Zhang, Zhiqi Wang, Neil Gong, Yuchen Yang — arXiv (cs.CR, 6 Oct 2026)

Identifies a security-utility asymmetry in lossy speculative decoding: efficiency gains cause disproportionate security degradation, with jailbreak and prompt injection attack success rising much faster than utility falls. SecureSD applies a stricter verification criterion to early draft-token positions (where the security loss originates), reducing jailbreak/injection attack success rates by up to 92.4% while retaining 99.8% of the decoding speedup and 98.4% of utility, across five benchmarks.

![Figure 7: Security-utility trade-off in speculative decoding before and after SecureSD](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.08678/x1.png)

*Figure 7: Attack success rate vs. decoding speedup for baseline speculative decoding methods versus SecureSD, showing that SecureSD recovers near-baseline security with minimal speedup loss.*

### 8. [**Localize-and-Detect: Auditing Task-Level Poisoning in Instruction-Tuned Models**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03960)

`AI security` `preprint` — Luze Sun, Cristina Nita-Rotaru, Alina Oprea — arXiv (cs.CR, 2 Oct 2026)

Proposes a two-stage black-box auditor for task-conditioned poisoning: Stage 1 ranks tasks by divergence between fine-tuned and base model output distributions; Stage 2 searches top-ranked tasks for biased content in fine-tuned responses. Evaluated on 216 poisoned models across two LLM families; white-box signals (attention activations, hidden states) do not outperform the black-box approach, making the method deployable without model internals access.

![Figure 8: Two-stage localize-and-detect auditing pipeline for task-level poisoning](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.03960/x1.png)

*Figure 8: Localize-and-detect pipeline — distribution divergence ranking (Stage 1) followed by biased-content detection (Stage 2), evaluated on 216 poisoned instruction-tuned models.*

### 9. [**PyINE: A Framework for Scalable Elicitation and Oversight via Code Execution**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04737)

`alignment` `preprint` — Pierre-Luc St-Charles, Yoshua Bengio et al. — arXiv (cs.AI, 3 Oct 2026)

Proposes using instrumented Python execution traces as a scalable ground-truth substrate for oversight research: ~1M deterministic execution traces with 500K+ matched LLM-generated code variants allow mechanical task generation without hand-annotation. RL-trained models improve substantially at predicting execution outcomes but still err when human-readable cues conflict with what the program actually does—a concrete, measurable instance of the deceptive cue problem in scalable oversight.

*Figure 9: PyINE pipeline: instrumented Python programs generate verifiable execution traces; LLM-generated code variants provide contrastive oversight signal at scale.*

### 10. [**From Expected Harmfulness to Likelihood: A Probabilistic Reformulation of Jailbreaking LLM Agents**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09973)

`AI security` `preprint` — Juanyang Xu, Zheng Wang, Xingyu Zhao, Siddartha Khastgir, Andi Zhang — arXiv (7 Oct 2026)

Shows that maximizing expected harmfulness and maximizing the likelihood of a harmful target output are equivalent by gradient identity, unifying two previously separate jailbreaking paradigms. The resulting OPUR method samples from a harmfulness-reweighted output distribution to guide likelihood-based input optimization and demonstrates improved jailbreak effectiveness against LLM agents on InjecAgent-style indirect-injection benchmarks.

![Figure 10: Gradient identity connecting expected harmfulness and target likelihood jailbreak objectives](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.09973/x1.png)

*Figure 10: OPUR method diagram—the harmfulness-reweighted sampling distribution bridges expected-harmfulness and likelihood-based jailbreak objectives through the shared gradient identity.*
