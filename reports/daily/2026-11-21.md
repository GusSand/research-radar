## 01 · [Attacks and Defenses in Decentralized Multi-Agent Systems with Hop-by-Hop Task Decomposition and Delegation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25056)

`AI security` `preprint` — Matthew D. Laws, Maria Lazou, Alina Oprea, Cristina Nita-Rotaru — arXiv preprint, cs.CR, 2026-11-21

A single malicious agent in a decentralized LLM multi-agent network can silently destroy all task outcomes, and a cryptographic execution-trace protocol catches 98% of such attacks with negligible latency overhead.

![Figure 2: Hop-by-hop system with Verified Execution Trace protocol showing how the cryptographic auditor attributes malicious agent behavior](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25056v1/agentnet_def.png)

*Figure 2: The Verified Execution Trace architecture — each agent signs its action log; the auditor traverses the graph to detect and attribute malicious behavior.*

The authors systematize integrity, availability, and confidentiality attacks on decentralized LLM systems where agents decompose and delegate tasks hop-by-hop. Empirically, one malicious agent causes up to 100% task failure, inflates token consumption by 7.7× and runtime by 3.2×, and exfiltrates up to 94% of relevant context from benign peers. Their defense attaches a cryptographic execution trace to each hop; an auditor then detects and correctly attributes 98% of attacks — including those that follow the protocol — with negligible latency cost. Evaluated on HiddenBench using gpt-6.1-sol and Qwen3.8-27B-FP8.

## 02 · [What the model knows but does not say: monitoring medical LLMs through verbalized thoughts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24994)

`mech-interp` `AI security` `preprint` — Frederik Hauke, Jan Clusmann, Hanna Kreutzer, et al. — arXiv preprint, cs.CL, 2026-11-21

An internal activation monitor that "reads the model's mind" before it answers catches adversarial injections in clinical settings that the answer channel misses — across two models, two attack types, and two modalities.

![Figure 1: Overview of two experiments showing verbalized thought monitoring versus answer-channel monitoring for detecting prompt injection in medical LLMs](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.24994v1/figures/OverviewFigurePromptInjection.png)

*Figure 1: The verbalized-thoughts pipeline — a natural-language autoencoder translates Gemma-3-27B activations into text; a blind judge reads the "thoughts" before seeing the final answer.*

The authors use a natural-language autoencoder to translate the internal activations of Gemma-3-27B into text (the "thoughts" channel), then route both channels to a judge that sees neither the input nor each other's output. Against two clinical injection objectives (omitting a pathology, covertly recommending a drug), two delivery modalities, and two attacker mechanisms, the thoughts channel flagged injected manipulation more often than the answer in all eight conditions (p<0.05 each). False-positive rates were near zero on clean inputs, and the injection-detection result replicated on Llama-3.3-70B. No retraining is required; the approach applies post-hoc to any model for which activations are accessible.

## 03 · [Separating Interpretability and Reconstruction in Sparse Autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25165)

`mech-interp` `preprint` — Anh Tien Nguyen, Dimitris Samaras, Mahdi S. Hosseini — arXiv preprint, cs.LG, 2026-11-21

Standard SAEs force one sparse code to simultaneously serve reconstruction and interpretability — goals that are not guaranteed to align — and splitting them into two branches yields +29% reconstruction and +34% monosemanticity across diverse settings.

![Figure 1: Comparison of standard SAE architecture versus DualSAE's dual-branch architecture separating sparse interpretable features from residual reconstruction](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25165v1/Figures/DualSAEvsSAE.jpg)

*Figure 1: Standard SAE (left) forces one sparse code to handle both tasks; DualSAE (right) adds a Residual Branch to absorb non-semantic information, freeing the Sparse Branch for interpretability.*

DualSAE adds a plug-in Residual Branch to a frozen standard SAE. The original Sparse Branch handles dominant semantics; the Residual Branch captures what the Sparse Branch omits. A decorrelation loss keeps the two branches' reconstruction contributions from overlapping, and a two-stage training schedule stabilizes the Sparse Branch before coupling in the Residual Branch. Across four dataset–encoder combinations (two vision, two language), DualSAE improves mean reconstruction fidelity by ~29% and monosemanticity score by ~34% relative to the SAE baseline. Downstream gains appear in topic modeling, hypothesis generation, and LLM activation steering experiments.

## 04 · [Blind Spots Travel, Knowledge Stays Home: What RL Inherits from an LLM Judge](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25191)

`alignment` `preprint` — Liang Yue, Eric A. Vance — arXiv preprint, cs.CL, 2026-11-21

An LLM judge's category-level blind spots bake into RL-trained models as refusals even when the model still knows the right answer — with just 12 wrong verdicts turning a 5% refusal rate on a category into 90%.

*Figure 1: Abstention rates on categories the judge misclassified during training versus control categories, showing that judge errors become model refusals not knowledge gaps.*

Across 814 RL training runs on 7-8B models learning to answer questions or abstain, judge knowledge about individual answers does not transfer (adding one corrected answer per 100 questions). But category-level judge beliefs do transfer dramatically: a judge that rejected 11 of 12 correct training answers about capitals of former states caused the RL model to refuse 98% of held-out questions in that category, while still answering 94% correctly when forced to respond. Fixing only those 12 verdicts moved refusal from 5% to 90%; at a lenient setting, 2 of 4 judges still cost 38–42% recall on the affected category. A self-agreement reward (most common untrained sample answer) matched answer-key reward and avoided the problem.

## 05 · [Pandora's Toolbox — Or Why Agent Safety Cannot Be Verified](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25325)

`alignment` `AI safety` `preprint` — Mario Franco, Carlos Gershenson — arXiv preprint, cs.AI, 2026-11-21

Coupling a fixed language model to a general computing device makes safety verification undecidable, and even under finite bounds it is PSPACE-complete — an important theoretical limit for the whole agent-safety enterprise.

*Figure 1: The agent loop diagram — the LM reads context and delegates to a Turing-complete device; the interaction generates undecidable safety predicates.*

The paper proves that for an agent consisting of a fixed LM plus a general computing device, no algorithm can decide whether a given agent will produce a dangerous program. With finite bounds on context, values, and runtime, the problem becomes PSPACE-complete even when the agent's description is fully known. Safety is also shown to be non-compositional: individually provably safe agents can produce unsafe behavior when combined. Guardrails, blacklists, and certifications can only provide imperfect approximations. The paper discusses practically useful relaxations, but the core result implies that formal safety certification for general tool-using LLM agents is intractable.

## 06 · [Does the Workspace Do the Work? A Causal Protocol for Verifying Global-Workspace Computation in a Language Model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26018)

`mech-interp` `preprint` — Diaoxuan He, Linqian Wu — arXiv preprint, cs.LG, 2026-11-21

*Figure 1: One swapped message separates relay from recombination — transplanting a message from a different input to a fixed receiver predicts three distinct answers depending on which computation the module performs.*

A causal transplant test — swapping the broadcast message from a different input while holding all else fixed — distinguishes three computations (ignore / relay / recombine) that accuracy and ablation alone cannot separate. On a trained AFGW architecture where modules communicate only through a learned workspace, the transplanted message produced the recombination-predicted answer in 97% of cases; the broadcast acts by redirecting what the receiving module retrieves from its own memory. The protocol is modular and extends to other workspace designs and to known interfaces of pretrained Transformers.

## 07 · [SoK: Assessing the Evolution of LLM Capability in Software Vulnerability Research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26601)

`AI security` `preprint` — Amine Lbath, Manan Suri, Aurelien Delaitre, Vadim Okun, Massih-Reza Amini, Dinesh Manocha — arXiv preprint, cs.CR, 2026-11-21

*Figure 1: Highest autonomy level by capability and access tier on direct evidence, with correctness category and date first shown on real software.*

This SoK introduces an autonomy-scale framework (modeled on driving automation levels) for rating LLM-agent vulnerability research capabilities, then applies it to a decade of public vulnerability data. Key findings: agents discovered, validated, and patched real vulnerabilities without human intervention before restricted frontier models arrived in 2026; open-weight models later reached comparable discovery and validation autonomy. Disclosed vulnerabilities more than doubled in 2026, with a growing critical/high share; severe decade-old bugs were reported several times more often. Time between vulnerability lifecycle stages has not notably shortened yet.

## 08 · [DriftGuard: Online Memory-Drift Detection via Causal Attribution for LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25583)

`AI security` `mech-interp` `preprint` — Licheng Pan, Yuzhou Chen, et al. — arXiv preprint, cs.AI, 2026-11-21

![Figure 1: Causal attribution pipeline for DriftGuard showing offline counterfactual replay for annotation and online risk model for real-time memory drift detection](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.25583v1/causal_attribution.png)

*Figure 1: DriftGuard's two-stage design — offline counterfactual replays produce Memory Drift Scores, which train an online risk model that flags drift-inducing memories at serving time without re-running the agent.*

DriftGuard addresses adversarial or misleading memories that redirect an LLM agent's execution path. Offline, counterfactual replays neutralize one memory at a time and score the resulting drift across action change, attack removal, violation removal, and task recovery, combined by majority voting into a Memory Drift Score. A lightweight risk model learns to estimate this score from pre-action information alone, enabling real-time flagging without expensive downstream replays at serving time. Content-level signals (perplexity, relevance) are shown insufficient to identify drift-inducing memories.

## 09 · [How Reliable Are Explanations of Language Models That Detect Insider Threats?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25796)

`mech-interp` `AI security` `preprint` — Suramya R. Angdembay — arXiv preprint, cs.LG, 2026-11-21

*Figure 1: The audit procedure — tipping radius measures how far the control's distance metric must shift before the removal effect disappears, quantifying explanation fragility.*

The paper audits feature-attribution explanations for LM-based insider-threat detectors trained on CERT workplace logs, testing them by removing the named neuron-activation features and comparing the score drop against a matched control. In 33 of 34 runs across seven models, the tipping radius — how far the metric must shift to erase the gap — is at most 18% of the distance between two standard metric choices, with random shifts of the same magnitude leaving the effect intact. The explained features also partly reflect the worker's identity rather than their actions, and the identified features change with the comparison group. The paper recommends pre-registering the control metric and reporting the tipping radius alongside any attribution result.

## 10 · [Backtracking Agent Applications Security Incidents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26525)

`AI security` `preprint` — Yicheng Cai, Taegyu Kim, Peng Liu — arXiv preprint, cs.CR, 2026-11-21

![Figure 1: Origin overview showing the cross-layer dependency graph (CLDG) that maps agent trajectory regions to function-call compromise entry points](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.26525v1/tool.png)

*Figure 1: Origin's cross-layer dependency graph — region-to-variable mappings link high-level agent functionality to low-level taint subgraph nodes, enabling query-driven CEP identification.*

Origin addresses the analyst's challenge of mapping high-level agent-trajectory descriptions to low-level application compromise entry points (CEPs). It builds a cross-layer dependency graph (CLDG) that groups functions by inferred regions of agent functionality and links them to variables in a taint-propagation subgraph, with a companion query language for structured backtracking. A region-inference algorithm determines the region-to-variable mapping automatically. In case studies covering five popular agent applications and eight attack incidents, analysts using Origin correctly identified all CEPs.
