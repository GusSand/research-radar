## 01 · [Feature Elicitation for SAE Steering of Deceptive Behaviour in Small LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20638)

`mech-interp` `alignment` `preprint` — J Korznikov — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Alignment faking — where a model appears compliant under one monitoring tier but would act differently otherwise — has resisted mechanistic fixes. This paper is the first to show that a SAE-based activation steering intervention can close the compliance gap, at least in Gemma-3-27B-IT, by steering features associated with deontological ethics.

![Figure 1: Feature elicitation pipeline — bag-of-words concept probing identifies deontological ethics features in SAE space for targeted steering](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20638v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Overview of the bag-of-words feature elicitation pipeline for identifying alignment-faking-relevant SAE features and applying targeted activation steering.*

A bag-of-words feature elicitation pipeline identifies concept-level steering directions in sparse autoencoder feature space corresponding to deontological ethical reasoning. Steering those features in Gemma-3-27B-IT reduces the compliance gap to statistical non-significance; the same approach was ineffective in LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct. Scratchpad trace analysis surfaced a novel behaviour the authors call "deceptive defanging" — the model degrades the harmfulness of its response while remaining superficially compliant, a previously unnamed failure mode of alignment training.

## 02 · [Screwtape: Adversarial Transcript Editing for Monitor Red-Teaming](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20941)

`AI security` `alignment` `preprint` — Gosha Kozhevnikov, Tyler Tracy — arXiv preprint (Redwood Research), Oct 2026

Monitors that watch agent action traces are central to AI control proposals, but their failure modes are rarely stress-tested. Screwtape automates adversarial editing of real agent transcripts to produce semantically coherent edits that carry out specified harms while evading the monitor — without ever running the edited actions.

![Figure 1: Screwtape pipeline — attacker LLM edits transcripts, verifier checks coherence, monitor evaluates, loop repeats](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20941v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: The Screwtape loop: attacker edits transcript to achieve a target harm, verifier filters incoherent or low-severity edits, monitor evaluates the result, attacker refines if blocked.*

The pipeline applied the attacker-verifier-monitor loop to 94 Claude Code and Codex transcripts from Redwood staff, running 252 total attack attempts. It produced 35 verified attacks that evaded the authors' own monitors. Six of those, confirmed valid by human review, also bypassed a held-out monitor from a frontier developer whose infrastructure the authors had no access to. The transfer of attacks to a black-box external monitor is the paper's sharpest result — it shows that a red-teamer operating outside a developer's deployment can still find monitor weaknesses in production systems.

## 03 · [LEASH: Uncertainty-Aware Weak-to-Strong Escalation and Oversight for Computer-Use Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22280)

`alignment` `AI security` `preprint` — Qingni Wang, Changdae Oh, Yuzhe Yang, Sharon Li, Jiachen Yang, Ang Li, Xin Eric Wang — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Running a frontier model at every step of a long-horizon computer-use task is prohibitively expensive, but small open-weight models fail too often. LEASH exploits the weak model's own hidden states to decide when to escalate to a strong model and then to monitor the strong model after handoff — without training a separate monitor.

![Figure 2: LEASH overview — weak model acts by default; latent uncertainty triggers escalation to strong model; shadow observer monitors strong model via weak model's hidden states](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22280v1/overview.png)

*Figure 2: LEASH architecture. The weak model's hidden-state uncertainty drives both the initial routing decision and the step-level escalation trigger; the shadow observer uses the same states to audit the strong (black-box) agent after handoff.*

LEASH uses three layers: initial task-level uncertainty for routing, step-level uncertainty during execution, and a shadow observer that monitors the strong black-box agent post-escalation. Uncertainty estimates compare current hidden states to those from the weak model's past successes and failures — no extra forward pass needed. On OSWorld across four backbones, LEASH beats standalone weak agents and outperforms random escalation by 6.55–11.94 points at matched escalation rates. With Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, LEASH reaches 73.66% task success at $0.41 per task, matching always-on Claude Opus 5 (72.90%) while cutting inference cost by 19.2%.

## 04 · [Effectiveness of Hidden-State Probes for Localizing Reasoning Failures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22046)

`mech-interp` `peer-reviewed` — Shubham Subhnil, Yannis Belkhiter, Erik Miehling, Adam Dahlgren Lindström, Elizabeth M. Daly — NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Interpretability as a Science (accepted)

Standard hidden-state probes are trained on final-answer correctness, so they inherit the same label for every type of failure — whether the model lacks capability, would succeed on retry, or failed at a single unrecoverable step. This NeurIPS workshop paper measures whether probes can actually separate these cases and localise where in the trace the failure happened.

![Figure 1: The three failure types — random error (retry succeeds), unrecoverable step failure, and incapability — compared against probe predictions](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22046v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: The three failure types in long reasoning traces: random error (a retry would succeed), a single unrecoverable step, and genuine incapability.*

On incorrect 8B-model traces, the confidence probe missed 81% of cases where one step made the trace unrecoverable and 47% of retry-recoverable failures; a differently trained probe reversed the ordering of those two failure types entirely, showing that results depend on training design rather than the underlying hidden states. Critically, no tested probe localised the failing step better than a baseline that simply uses trace length. The finding constrains the use of probes as runtime diagnostics for reasoning LLMs: detecting that an answer is wrong is feasible, but knowing why or where it failed is not yet achievable.

## 05 · [CAT-alyzing Honesty: Continuous Adversarial Training for Transferable LLM Honesty](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22292)

`alignment` `preprint` — Yuan-Chen Chang, Niklas Herbster, Gauthier Gidel, Tommaso Tosato — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

CATCH (Continuous Adversarial Training for Consistent Honesty) trains models to be honest by adapting the adversarial-training objective originally developed for robust refusal. Using only a few hundred training targets, it reduces dishonesty under MASK, increases confessions in Among Us, and improves hidden-behaviour discovery in AuditBench — outperforming prompting and SFT baselines while matching activation steering on honesty and beating it on utility.

![Figure 1: CATCH training loop — adversarial pressure pushes model toward honest answers and away from dishonest ones under pressure](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22292v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: CATCH training schema. Adversarial examples that elicit dishonest outputs are generated and used to fine-tune the model away from dishonesty while preserving general capability via curated utility data.*

## 06 · [PROBE: Learning to Audit Policy Compliance in Tool-Using LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22124)

`alignment` `AI security` `preprint` — Kshitij Mishra, Abhijith Sharma, Nils Lukas, Salem Lahlou — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Existing evaluation of LLM agents checks capability or harmfulness but not whether agents silently violate predefined operational policies (pricing, privacy, confirmation, and safety rules) during otherwise valid tool use. PROBE trains RL auditors to discover such violations through multi-turn, tool-mediated dialogue formalized as a two-player Markov game.

![Figure 1: PROBE overview — RL auditor probes a deployed agent through multi-turn tool-mediated dialogue to uncover policy violations](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22124v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: PROBE architecture. An RL-trained auditor issues queries and interprets tool outputs to discover policy violations that single-turn probing would miss.*

Training combines violation-discovery reward, behavioral plausibility, conversation-progress, and policy-diversity terms; the paper evaluates GRPO, DPO, and GFlowNet optimization. Auditors trained with PROBE find substantially broader and higher-yield policy violations than zero-shot adversaries with the same architecture — even when the auditor model is much smaller than the target agent — suggesting a co-evolutionary loop where auditors expose failures and agents improve from that feedback.

## 07 · [Shortcut Sensitivity in Agent-Safety Evaluations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22243)

`alignment` `AI security` `preprint` — Hasan Demirkiran, Jens Ernstberger — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Guards that supervise AI agent tool-use can be evaluated against datasets that contain cues predicting labels independently of the intended safety property; those same cues can shift deployed guard decisions. This paper introduces a three-level shortcut audit framework and applies it to LinuxArena and two TS-Bench-derived datasets.

![Figure 1: Three-level shortcut audit — label predictability, observational guard association, paired decision sensitivity](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.22243v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: The three-level audit: label predictability (can the label be predicted from a cue alone?), observational association (does the cue correlate with guard scores?), and paired decision sensitivity (does the cue shift guard decisions in controlled interventions?).*

Domain holdout drops released TS-Guard exact-tool F1 from 0.901 to 0.495; rewriting the `<INFORMATION>` marker lowers blocking by 8.33 percentage points while opaque tool aliases raise it by 11.46 points; both effects survive a formatting-placebo control. The authors recommend domain group holdouts, evaluation-unit metrics, and placebo-controlled paired interventions as standard methodology for agent-safety benchmarks.

## 08 · [StepSafe: Learning Timely Intervention with Context-Aware Safety Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20960)

`AI security` `alignment` `preprint` — Siyi Gu, Kunhan Gao, Chao Peter Yang, Sukanta Ganguly, Rex Ying, Arman Cohan — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Safety in agentic systems is inherently sequential: whether an action is harmful depends on prior steps and downstream consequences, so current monitors that rate actions in isolation miss the intervention window. StepSafe is trained with ASPO (Adaptive Stage-Aligned Policy Optimization) to intervene at the right point in a trajectory; StepSafe-4B achieves Youden's J of 53.0 across five agent-safety benchmarks, against 46.2 for the prior SOTA.

![Figure 2: StepSafe ASPO framework — stage-aligned policy optimization with adaptive feedback self-distillation for timely intervention](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20960v1/figures/framework_connected_v1/main_framework.png)

*Figure 2: The ASPO training framework. Stage-aligned policy optimization rewards the appropriate decision at each step stage; adaptive feedback self-distillation uses the model's own false-alarm annotations to tighten the policy.*

## 09 · [INFUSE: A Unified Surface Benchmark for Indirect Prompt Injection in Agentic Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20776)

`AI security` `preprint` — Thuy Dung Nguyen, Jingyi Guo, Rana Seda Dubauskas, Sreepriya Damuluru, Taylor T. Johnson, Kevin Leach — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Existing indirect prompt injection (IPI) evaluations use isolated malicious strings and single-step tool calls; INFUSE covers 40 realistic long-horizon agent tasks with 56 attack scenarios across five threat categories, totalling 41,824 adversarial trajectories and 720 benign reference runs across 19 LLMs.

![Figure 1a: INFUSE benchmark overview — attacks injected through interaction surfaces during long-horizon agent workflows](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20776v1/overview_small.png)

*Figure 1a: INFUSE benchmark structure. Attacks are injected through surfaces encountered during task execution, not only in the initial prompt.*

Mean ASR is 19.6% (LLM-based) and 15.4% (heuristic); per-model ASR ranges from 3.2% to 41.9%. Prompt-level instructional hardening cuts mean ASR from 58.2% to 14.3%, but with model-dependent utility trade-offs. Existing guard models provide limited protection, motivating task-level defenses.

## 10 · [From Detection to Decision: Calibration and Deferral for Prompt-Injection Detection](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20907)

`AI security` `preprint` — Qianlong Lan, Anuj Kaul, Vinothini Pandurangan, Indranil Sanyal — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Deployed LLM applications must allow, block, or escalate inputs — not just classify them — yet prompt-injection defenses are almost always benchmarked as binary detectors. This paper evaluates four systems (Jev, Llama Prompt Guard 2 22M/86M, and an LLM judge) on 47,611 held-out examples covering distribution shift, obfuscation, and hard-benign inputs.

![Figure 1: Frozen evaluation pipeline — detect → calibrate → decide with allow/block/defer policy](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.20907v1/Frozen_Evaluation_Pipeline_Diagram.png)

*Figure 1: The evaluation design. Detection, calibration, and operational decision policy are measured separately under a frozen evaluation protocol.*

The LLM judge leads AUROC at 0.897 (vs. Jev 0.871, PG2-86M 0.757, PG2-22M 0.714). Temperature scaling improves calibration but not discrimination. At a 1% false-negative-rate target, every gate-reviewer policy required >91.8% escalation rates, showing that current systems cannot both enforce safety and maintain low deferral in production without an expensive reviewer pipeline.
