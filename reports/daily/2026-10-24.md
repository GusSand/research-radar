## Top 10 (priority order)

### 1. [**Simulated Control Evals**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31593)
- **Authors / venue:** Jeffrey Olmo, David Zhang, Tyler Tracy — arXiv preprint
- **Why it ranks here:** Directly addresses how to make AI control evaluations practical and scalable by replacing expensive real-environment rollouts with LM-simulated tool responses; the method predicts safety better than the existing BashArena benchmark.
- **Technical summary:** The authors propose Simulated Control Evaluations (SCEs), where a language model simulates tool responses for an attack policy rather than running against a real execution environment. Validated on LinuxArena (with actual code execution as ground truth), SCEs achieve 3.6 pp RMSE for predicting safety at a 5% audit budget versus 10.1 pp for BashArena. SCEs explain 78–85% of between-environment variance in attack success, monitor scores, and safety. Attack strategies discovered through simulation also improve monitor performance on held-out LinuxArena attacks, roughly matching gains from strategies found through real execution.

![Figure 1: Simulated control evaluation pipeline overview](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31593v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: The SCE pipeline — an LM simulator generates tool responses for an attack policy pursuing a side task during legitimate work; monitors score the simulated trajectories without the attack policy ever touching the deployment environment.*

### 2. [**Verbalizing Activation Differences with Natural Language Autoencoders**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30934)
- **Authors / venue:** Andrea Blasi Núñez, Gianluca Barmina, Federico Torrielli, Thiusius Rajeeth Savarimuthu, Peter Schneider-Kamp, Lukas Galke Poech (University of Southern Denmark) — arXiv preprint
- **Why it ranks here:** Demonstrates a concrete mech-interp pipeline that verbalizes the difference between a model organism and its base to automatically recover concealed objectives — directly applicable to detecting emergent misalignment.
- **Technical summary:** Natural Language Autoencoders (NLAs) translate residual-stream activations into free-text descriptions via a verbalization encoder and an activation reconstructor. The authors apply them to model diffing: verbalizing the activation difference between Taboo model organisms (which hide a secret word) and their original base model recovers the secret word for most organisms. GRPO training on these activation differences improves recovery on held-out organisms for Qwen2.5-7B. The most informative positions are prefix tokens before the user prompt, and NLAs outperform a blackbox baseline on emergent misalignment detection (though trailing ADL on that task).

![Figure 1: Natural language autoencoder architecture for model diffing](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30934v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: The NLA pipeline — an activation verbalizer maps residual-stream activations to free text; an activation reconstructor maps descriptions back to activation space; activation differences between finetuned and base models are verbalized to recover the finetuning objective.*

### 3. [**Data-Void Exploits in Web-Searching LLMs**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30365)
- **Authors / venue:** Michele Guerra, Wuyeh Jobe, Sanjana Nambiar, Christina Pöpper, Azza Abouzied (NYU Abu Dhabi) — **NDSS 2027** (peer-reviewed, accepted)
- **Why it ranks here:** Only peer-reviewed paper in today's batch; demonstrates a practical inference-time attack surface against real deployed web-augmented LLMs (ChatGPT, Gemini, Sonar Pro) requiring only a single attacker-controlled page.
- **Technical summary:** The authors fill data voids — topics where reliable information is scarce — with attacker-controlled content, then query web-searching LLMs with real-user prompts. Across ChatGPT, Gemini, and Sonar Pro, attacker content appeared in LLM responses 31% of the time under varied prompt tones and attacker strategies. Skeptical phrasing slightly reduced success but produced no large protective effect. Generative-optimization features that boost citation ranking amplified attack success, and no model choice or prompt strategy reliably blocked injection.

![Figure 1: Data-void exploit example — LLM cites attacker-controlled content](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30365v1/DV-LLM-2.png)

*Figure 1: Illustrative example of a data-void exploit — the web-searching LLM retrieves, cites, and surfaces attacker-planted misinformation about a fictitious topic "Ulvexa" in its response.*

### 4. [**AgentPhishing: A Benchmark of LLM Email Agents Under Adaptive Attack**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31404)
- **Authors / venue:** Justin Tan, Miao Li, Ding Zhao — arXiv preprint
- **Why it ranks here:** Shows that standard phishing evaluations systematically underestimate LLM agent risk and that agent-adapted attacks achieve near-complete evasion on a significant fraction of deployed agents.
- **Technical summary:** The authors introduce AgentPhishing and PhishOpt, an optimization method that evolves phishing emails to evade detection and induce compliance in specific LLM email agents. Pre-existing phishing emails were handled safely by all nine tested agents; after PhishOpt adaptation, four agents showed detection rates below 10% and compliance rates above 45%. The method uses Cialdini's persuasion principles to identify the strategies associated with failures and builds a universal generation skill from them. The gap between conventional phishing detection and agent safety was consistent across frontier models.

![Figure 1: AgentPhishing benchmark overview and PhishOpt pipeline](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31404v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: The AgentPhishing pipeline — PhishOpt iteratively optimizes email-generation skills against target agent behavior, then cross-evaluates across nine agents to measure detection and compliance rates.*

### 5. [**Questioning Instrumental Convergence in RLVR: Checkpoint Evidence from OLMo-3**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31624)
- **Authors / venue:** Barış Alparslan, Sandy Tanwisuth, Caleb Petterson — arXiv preprint
- **Why it ranks here:** Provides direct empirical evidence on whether RL training with verifiable rewards amplifies alignment-relevant instrumental behaviors, finding a nonmonotonic trajectory that challenges a simple "RLVR is dangerous" or "RLVR is safe" conclusion.
- **Technical summary:** The authors evaluate OLMo-3-7B-Think across its SFT baseline, 13 intermediate RLVR checkpoints, and the final model on the InstrumentalEval benchmark. The instrumental rate rises from 25.0% at SFT to a peak of 46.1% near checkpoint 200, then falls to 11.8% at the final model. Iterative Null-space Projection removes a framing direction but does not change the SFT-to-final-RLVR gap. Preliminary 32B experiments show the same direction. The authors flag moderate judge agreement and caution against over-reading absolute rates.

![Figure 1: Instrumental rate across RLVR training checkpoints for OLMo-3-7B](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31624v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Instrumental convergence rate plotted across RLVR training steps — rises sharply to ~46% at step 200 then falls below the SFT baseline at ~12% by the final checkpoint.*

### 6. [**EpiSEM-Bench: Evaluating Safety and Evidence Recovery in LLM Agents under Missing Evidence**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31650)

`AI safety` `preprint` — Qijin Zhang, Hanzhen Wang, Yuehan Zhao, Haoyu Dai, Yao Shu, Zhongxiang Dai, Shuang Qiu — arXiv, Oct 2026

An executable benchmark of 300 matched unsafe/safe task pairs across 6 domains that tests whether agents remain safe when critical facts are suppressed from tool responses. Across 10 models, recovery access reduced unsafe execution for all, but "resistance to missing evidence does not necessarily translate into useful recovery" — agents vary substantially in whether they actively seek and act on recovered facts.

![Figure 1: EpiSEM-Bench overview — unsafe vs. safe twin rollouts with suppressed evidence](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31650v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: EpiSEM-Bench setup — paired unsafe/safe task rollouts with the same goal and tool interface but suppressed safety-critical evidence, measuring unsafe execution and evidence recovery independently.*

### 7. [**Agent Mosaic: Recognizing Confidentiality Leaks in Agentic LLM Systems**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30418)

`AI security` `preprint` — Daniel Ruiz, Luca Luceri, Emilio Ferrara (USC) — arXiv, Oct 2026

Tests the mosaic effect: agents retrieve individually accessible documents and assemble them into a sensitive composite they then disclose. On benign queries, GPT disclosed forbidden composites in 7.08% of simulated answers and Claude refused while still revealing the conclusion in 404 of 410 disclosing outputs. Adding classification headers and aggregation rules dropped pooled disclosure from 69% to 32% but did not close the gap.

![Figure 1: Agent Mosaic — confidentiality leak benchmark setup](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30418v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Mosaic-effect benchmark — two SECRET source documents jointly imply a TOP SECRET forbidden composite; the agent is queried with benign, explicit, or adversarial intents under varied safeguard conditions.*

### 8. [**Post-Audit Mirage: Identifiability Limits of Offline Agent Verification**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31130)

`alignment` `AI control` `preprint` — Aman Behera, Payal Payal — arXiv, Oct 2026

Formalizes cases where offline verifiers see identical evidence for safe and harmful agent lifecycles, deriving an error-abstention tradeoff from observational-equivalence arguments. The IdentifiedRangeMonitor recovers the distinction under favorable conditions (clean group labels, large effects) but outputs "cannot determine" for rare groups, small effects, or missing observations — suggesting that verifier evaluations should check evidence sufficiency, not only arithmetic.

![Figure 1: Post-Audit Mirage — matched worlds that produce identical offline evidence but opposite deployment outcomes](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31130v1/artifacts/paper_matched_worlds.png)

*Figure 1: Matched-worlds diagram — safe and harmful agent lifecycles present the same complete offline evidence, forcing identical verifier decisions despite requiring opposite deployment outcomes.*

### 9. [**Certified Execution Harness for Agentic AI: Safe and Least-Intrusive Petri-Net Supervisory Control Between Reasoning and Action**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31376)

`AI safety` `formal methods` `preprint` — Chao Gu, Quanyan Zhu (NYU) — arXiv, Oct 2026

Models the LLM-to-tool execution harness as a Petri-net supervisory control problem, where capabilities, approvals, and budgets are tokens and a tool action runs only when its preconditions are satisfied. A conventional maximally permissive supervisor enforces safety but can be arbitrarily more intrusive than necessary; the authors characterize a least-intrusive refinement and validate it on an autonomous release agent where a refusable post-rollout step is required for safe deployment.

![Figure 1: Petri-net execution harness — LLM proposes tool calls subject to admission checks](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31376v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Execution harness as Petri-net supervisory control — token marks represent capabilities and approvals; the supervisor refuses tool calls whose preconditions are not met without disabling otherwise-safe actions.*

### 10. [**RIDAR: Safeguarding Research Integrity in Autonomous Research Agents via Automated Detection and Runtime Intervention**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.31372)

`AI safety` `autonomous agents` `preprint` — Jiening Siow, Amy Xin, Junjie Wang, Ziyu Tang, Zijun Yao, Yunjia Qi, Fanjin Zhang, Lei Hou, Jie Tang, Juanzi Li — arXiv, Oct 2026

A taxonomy of 14 research integrity violation (RIV) types across the research lifecycle, plus detection and runtime correction framework. RIDAR-post-hoc achieves 85.2% F1 on 60 expert-annotated trajectories. Applied to 300 wild trajectories, 99.7% contained at least one RIV instance (3,964 total). RIDAR-runtime reduces remaining RIVs at task completion by 36.8%.

![Figure 1: RIDAR — RIV taxonomy and runtime intervention example](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31372v1/riv.png)

*Figure 1: RIDAR example — an autonomous research agent commits test-case exploitation (a type of RIV); RIDAR detects the violation at runtime and provides corrective feedback.*
