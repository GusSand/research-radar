## 01 · [Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09867)

- **Authors / venue:** Alexander Panfilov, David Schmotz, Ilia Shumailov, Luca Beurer-Kellner, Joachim Schaeffer, Ameya Prabhu, Jonas Geiping, Maksym Andriushchenko — arXiv preprint (August 10, 2026)
- **Why it ranks here:** Disclosed a cryptographic-protocol flaw shared across OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google — encrypted CoT blocks are interchangeable across sessions, users, and models within a provider's ecosystem. Mass-decoded 704 real user privacy artifacts (62 API keys, 33 passwords) from 6,708 public trajectories in two API calls per block. Immediate practical impact.
- **Technical summary:** Major providers return hidden chain-of-thought as encrypted opaque blocks; the client re-sends these blocks each turn. The authors discover the blocks are fully compatible across sessions and models within the same provider's ecosystem, enabling a decryption jailbreak: inject an encrypted block from a strong, safety-trained model into a weaker, less-safeguarded model in the same ecosystem and instruct it to decode and output the block verbatim. Four abuse paths are demonstrated: (1) model distillation — steal proprietary reasoning for training a local model; (2) private data extraction — public agent trajectories containing other users' encrypted thinking blocks can be decoded, yielding 704 artifacts (62 API keys, 33 passwords, 24 access tokens, 7 private keys) from 6,708 real trajectories; (3) harmful content recovery — content hidden behind a safe visible answer is recoverable from the thinking block; (4) prompt injection hiding — malicious instructions can be embedded in opaque reasoning, invisible to monitors that only inspect visible output.

![Figure 1: Cross-session encrypted CoT block injection attack flow — weaker model decodes encrypted thinking blocks from stronger model](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.09867/x1.png)

*Figure 1: The encrypted thinking block from a strong model (left) is injected into a weaker model's session (right), which decodes and outputs the trace verbatim — demonstrating cross-session compatibility used as a decryption oracle.*

## 02 · [Diffusion LLMs as Targets and Adversaries: Mechanistic Safety Exploits](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07430)

- **Authors / venue:** Elena Dumitrescu, Gert Lek, Lydia Y. Chen, Jérémie Decouchant — arXiv preprint (August 7, 2026)
- **Why it ranks here:** Highest-priority dLLM + mech-interp + security crossover in this window. Shows DLLMs inherit the same sparse safety-neuron footprint as their AR predecessors — and that pruning or steering those neurons breaks safety in both the source and the derived diffusion model. Transfer attacks achieve >86% ASR.
- **Technical summary:** Safety alignment in autoregressive LLMs concentrates in sparse "safety neuron" subsets. DLLMs initialized from AR predecessors (LLaDA←Llama-3; Dream, Fast-dLLM←Qwen2.5) inherit the identical mechanistic safety footprint. Self-pruning (calibrate safety neurons from the DLLM itself, then prune) raises ASR from 2.6%→73.8% on LLaDA and 1.9%→86.6% on Dream; transfer pruning (map safety neurons from an AR source with no DLLM calibration data) achieves 73.2% on Dream and 86.3% on Fast-dLLM. SN-Guided Diffusion, a fully offline black-box jailbreak, uses a weighted safety-neuron loss to steer the iterative denoising away from safety-triggering subspaces, achieving transfer ASR of 77.1% on Llama-3-8B-Instruct, 86.9% on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and 74.3% vs Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite.

![Figure 1: DLLM safety neuron transfer — AR predecessor safety neuron map pruned in derived diffusion model](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07430/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Safety neurons identified in the AR predecessor (Qwen2.5) are mapped to the derived DLLM (Dream/Fast-dLLM); pruning those neurons raises ASR from <2% to >86%, demonstrating that DLLM safety inherits and is bounded by its AR source's mechanistic footprint.*

## 03 · [Intrinsic Structure: Spectral Identifiability for Mechanistic Interpretability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10172)

- **Authors / venue:** Ashim Dhor, Pin-Yu Chen — arXiv preprint (August 10, 2026)
- **Why it ranks here:** First identifiability theorem for a mechanistic-interpretability primitive — proves that the transformer's intrinsic spectrum exists, is coordinate-free, and is recoverable from data at a provably optimal rate. Directly addresses the reproducibility crisis where SAEs trained on identical activations with different seeds return different features.
- **Technical summary:** Sparse autoencoders recover materially different features from the same activations depending on random seeds and widths, with no prior theory able to distinguish structural from incidental variability. This paper treats the forward pass as a controlled dynamical system (depth = time) and lifts it via the Koopman operator to a finite linear realization whose spectrum is a coordinate-free, basis-independent property of the network. Main results: (1) the spectrum is recoverable from M calibration samples at rate M^{-1/2} up to permutation — the first identifiability theorem for a mech-interp primitive, with a matching minimax lower bound; (2) a dissociation theorem: whenever the realization is non-normal (the generic case for deep transformers), the directions carrying activation variance and the directions propagating information across depth cannot coincide — explaining why PCA-based probes fail to capture causally active circuits. Spectrum convergence is empirically validated on GPT-2 Small, Gemma-2-2B, and Qwen3-8B-Base, attaining the predicted M^{-1/2} exponent.

![Figure 1: Koopman spectrum of transformer forward pass — coordinate-free eigenvalue convergence on GPT-2, Gemma-2-2B, Qwen3](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10172/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Empirical Koopman spectrum convergence on GPT-2 Small, Gemma-2-2B, and Qwen3-8B-Base; eigenvalues converge at the predicted M^{-1/2} rate, establishing the first provably optimal identifiability result for a mechanistic interpretability primitive.*

## 04 · [Detecting Safety Training Modification in Language Models via Activation Analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05578)

`mech-interp` `AI security` `IEEE Access (peer-reviewed)` — Glen Messenger (Google Cloud) · IEEE Access vol. 14, pp. 91723–91737, 2026

AMS (Activation-based Model Scanner) detects when language model safety training has been removed or weakened by measuring the geometric structure of safety-relevant concepts in activation space. Safety training creates measurable separation between harmful and benign content classes; abliteration, uncensored fine-tuning, and reversion to base each produce characteristic collapses or rotations of this geometry. Validated across 14 model configurations spanning 4 architecture families (Llama, Gemma, Qwen, Mistral) and 4 safety-modification categories; leave-one-out cross-validation achieves 71% accuracy (10/14), with bootstrap 95% CIs having median width 3.4σ.

![Figure 1: AMS geometric separation of harmful/benign classes in activation space across safety-modified models](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.05578/x1.png)

*Figure 1: AMS measures the angular separation of harmful vs. benign activation clusters; abliterated and uncensored models show characteristic geometric collapse, enabling classification of safety-modification type without access to training data.*

## 05 · [Thinking vs. NoThinking: Towards Interpreting Reasoning Mechanisms of Large Language Models via Sparse Autoencoders](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08168)

`mech-interp` `preprint` — Bo Cheng, Qiaolin Lu, Yi Chang, Yuan Wu — arXiv, August 8, 2026

Top-K SAEs are trained on intermediate representations of DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and used to compare the model's Thinking (CoT) and NoThinking (direct-answer) modes across three difficulty levels of math problems. Thinking mode activates sparse, high-intensity features driving verbal deduction, with the activation pattern stable regardless of problem difficulty. NoThinking mode shows an adaptive and diffuse pattern that prioritizes symbolic manipulation over verbal reasoning, scaling in complexity with problem difficulty. Provides the first SAE-based mechanistic account of how reasoning modes distribute computation differently within a shared network.

![Figure 1: SAE feature activation comparison — sparse high-intensity Thinking vs. adaptive diffuse NoThinking across difficulty levels](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08168/x1.png)

*Figure 1: SAE feature activation magnitude and density for Thinking (left) vs. NoThinking (right) mode across easy/medium/hard math tasks; Thinking mode = sparse-high, NoThinking = diffuse-adaptive.*

## 06 · [ToolHazard: Scaling Adversarial Environments for Security Evaluation and Alignment of LLM-based Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11878)

`AI security` `preprint` — arXiv, August 12, 2026 (Work in Progress)

Current security evaluations of tool-using LLM agents rely on manually implemented environments, limiting coverage to a handful of domains; stochastic LLM-based tool simulation makes reproducibility difficult; predefined injection locations miss adaptive attacker strategies. ToolHazard addresses all three: an Environment Simulator synthesizes executable stateful environments from seed domain descriptions; an Attacker Agent discovers viable injection points within those environments and generates environment-specific payloads; a User Simulator constructs state-grounded long-horizon tasks to stress agents under realistic workflows. ToolHazard-Bench is derived from these components for scalable, reproducible stress-testing across diverse environmental attacks.

![Figure 1: ToolHazard framework — Environment Simulator, Attacker Agent, User Simulator pipeline](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11878/x1.png)

*Figure 1: ToolHazard three-component pipeline: Environment Simulator generates executable stateful environments; Attacker Agent discovers injection points and synthesizes payloads; User Simulator creates long-horizon tasks; arrows show data flow into ToolHazard-Bench.*

## 07 · [REDAgentBench: Executable Red Teaming and Faithful Measurement of LLM Agent Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10669)

`AI security` `preprint` — Zixing Chen et al. — arXiv, August 11, 2026

Existing agent safety evaluations reduce agent safety to a single attack success rate (ASR), collapsing exposure, execution, observation, and adjudication into one number. REDAgentBench derives attacks from explicit safety constraints and associated vulnerability descriptions, runs them in isolated service sandboxes, and verifies harmful effects from service receipts and final-state changes — distinguishing actual policy violations from incomplete execution and false-positive detection. Benchmark design enables faithful measurement of where in the agent execution pipeline a safety failure actually occurs.

![Figure 1: REDAgentBench evaluation decomposition — exposure, execution, observation, adjudication stages](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10669/x1.png)

*Figure 1: REDAgentBench pipeline breaks ASR into exposure/execution/observation/adjudication phases, verified against service receipts and final-state changes rather than LLM self-report.*

## 08 · [On Understanding, Identifying, and Mitigating Vulnerabilities in Agentic Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10530)

`AI security` `preprint` — arXiv, August 11, 2026 (PRISMA 2020 systematic review)

Systematic literature review across 6 databases: 743 records screened, 85 papers retained (2023–2025). Attack research outpaces defense work by 3.9:1. Perception-layer vulnerabilities (prompt injection, jailbreaking, adversarial perturbations) dominate at 66% of papers; action-layer vulnerabilities (tool misuse, code injection, sandbox escape) appear in only 4.7%. Provides a structured taxonomy of vulnerability types by agent architecture layer and identifies the most under-defended attack surfaces.

![Figure 1: Taxonomy of agentic LLM vulnerabilities by layer — perception 66% vs. action 4.7%](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10530/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Distribution of 85 reviewed papers by vulnerability layer; perception-layer attacks (prompt injection, jailbreaking) account for 66%, while action-layer defenses remain critically under-studied at 4.7%.*

## 09 · [Convergent Detour Hijacking: Task-Preserving Resource Amplification in Skill-Based LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12273)

`AI security` `preprint` — Junliang Liu, Ruoyu Li, Wenxin Tang, Jingyu Xiao, Zhenyu Liu, Jingheng Xu, Laizhong Cui — arXiv, ~August 12, 2026

Introduces Convergent Detour Hijacking, a novel attack class against skill-based LLM agents: the agent is hijacked to execute attacker-controlled resource-amplifying subroutines while still converging on and completing the original user task. Because the task is completed, completion-based safety monitors and user-facing audits do not flag the attack. The "detour" is invisible in the final output; detection requires auditing intermediate resource consumption, not task outcomes.

![Figure 1: Convergent Detour Hijacking — task-preserving agent hijack with amplified resource side-channel](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.12273/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Normal skill execution (top) vs. Convergent Detour Hijack (bottom) — both converge on the same final task output, but the hijacked path executes attacker-controlled resource-amplifying subroutines undetected by completion-based monitors.*

## 10 · [From Interpretability to Control: Insights from Six Years of the TrustNLP Workshop](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11171)

`mech-interp` `AI security` `preprint` — arXiv, August 2026

Meta-analysis synthesizing 144 proceedings papers from six years of the TrustNLP workshop across six trust dimensions: fairness, robustness, privacy, transparency, accountability, and safety. Documents the field's trajectory from post-hoc interpretability of static classification models to mechanistic understanding and proactive control of generative systems. Identifies mechanistic interpretability and steering as the dominant emerging direction, with transparency and safety converging as joint research targets.
