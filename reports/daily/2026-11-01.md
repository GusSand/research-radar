### 01 · [**Loss is Not Behavior: A Unified Output-Space Analysis of Gradient-Based Machine Unlearning**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38784)

`NeurIPS 2026` `mech-interp` `unlearning` — Xingjian Zhao, Mohammad Mohammadi Amiri, Malik Magdon-Ismail — **NeurIPS 2026 (accepted)**

**Why it ranks here:** The paper exposes a fundamental diagnostic gap: gradient-based unlearning methods can converge to similar loss values while producing arbitrarily different output behaviors, meaning loss alone cannot certify that a forget set is truly erased — directly relevant for auditing deployed LLMs.

*Figure 1: Two gradient-based unlearning methods can reach the same forget-set loss while diverging in output behavior. The gap is governed by two leverage matrices — the "forget self-leverage" and the "retain cross-leverage" — derived from the retrained model's geometry.*

Zhao et al. show that the gap between an unlearned model's outputs and those of a retain-only retrained model has a closed-form structure in linear regression, then extend this to nonlinear networks via a generalized Gauss-Newton approximation with local linearity. The key insight is that all gradient-based unlearning methods correspond to a three-parameter family (geometry, reweighting, projection), and two leverage matrices derived from the retrained model's geometry govern how the forget residual propagates into both forget and retain outputs. Existing methods account for this geometry only partially. The framework yields precomputable leverage diagnostics that let practitioners audit forget sets and predict which type of unlearning is likely to succeed — a directly actionable tool for practitioners seeking to certify concept erasure.

### 02 · [**Exposure Is Not Commitment: Double-Stratified Evaluation of Latent Monitors for Tool-Using Agents**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37679)

`NeurIPS 2026 Workshop` `mech-interp` `agent-security` — Rishabh Sinha — **NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Agents in the Wild (AIWILD), poster**

**Why it ranks here:** Explains why published probe results on agent safety have been so inconsistent — naive training contrasts learn exposure (did the agent see an injection?) rather than commitment (will it comply?) — and shows that the correct stratification recovers a probe AUROC of 0.912.

*Figure 1: Naive probes trained on "unsafe vs. everything else" mostly learn whether the agent was exposed to an injection — they flag nearly all exposed trajectories regardless of whether the agent complied. Retraining on exposed-only cases recovers a genuine commitment signal up to two steps before the unsafe action (AUROC 0.853–0.912).*

Sinha proposes a "double-stratification" protocol that separates the evaluation and training populations by injection exposure and behavioral commitment. On the Qwen AgentDojo setup, naively trained probes achieve near-perfect AUROC separating exposed from unexposed trajectories (1.000 for probes, 0.908 for TF-IDF text), but barely predict which exposed trajectories commit to the unsafe action (near 0.5). Retrained on exposed-only cases, probes reach AUROC 0.853 on Qwen, 0.896 on Mistral-7B, and 0.912 on OLMo-2-7B, two steps before the unsafe tool call — the maximum the three-step trajectories allow. This suggests that previously published negative results on pre-action safety probes may largely reflect training-contrast selection rather than a genuine absence of representable commitment signal.

### 03 · [**When Skills Tell Agents Not to Ask: A Large-Scale Study of Oversight Directives in Agent Skills**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37734)

`preprint` `alignment` `agent-security` — Timofey Sanko, Yuan Tian — arXiv cs.CR preprint, 27 Oct 2026

**Why it ranks here:** The first large-scale empirical measurement of oversight erosion in agent skill ecosystems: roughly 1 in 17 of 1.53 million real-world skills contains directives that explicitly tell agents to skip approval or proceed without checking in.

![Figure 2: Study pipeline — from skills collection through codebook labeling and detection](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.37734v1/workflow.png)

*Figure 2: The study pipeline: 1.53M GitSkills crawled, split into semantic units, hand-labeled with a polarity/mechanism/scope codebook, and used to train a large-scale detector.*

Sanko and Tian analyzed 1.53 million English skills from GitSkills, labeling "oversight-loosening directives" — instructions that tell an agent to proceed without approval, skip checks, or continue without checking in — across 2,097 hand-annotated units. About 1 in 17 skills (~88,000) contains such a directive; 70.1% of these apply unconditionally (not scoped to CI or automated contexts). Skills that run shell code, install packages, or use sudo have 1.7–1.8× the odds of containing a loosening directive. Critically, loosening concentrates in a small upstream cluster: 2.4% of skill families account for half of all loosening-carrying copies, meaning a handful of widely-forked skills contaminate large fractions of the ecosystem. Existing open-source scanners detect at most 46% of directives (precision below 0.4); a codebook-guided LLM reaches 61% recall with 0.71 precision, pointing toward a deployable monitoring approach.

### 04 · [**CARI: Cross-Boundary Adversarial Runtime Injection in Coding-Agent Workflows**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.38799)

`preprint` `security` — Jie Fu, Shuang Wu, Ashish Kundu, Wendy Hui Wang, Yuan Hong — arXiv cs.CR/cs.SE preprint, 28 Oct 2026

![Figure 1: CARI framework — a peer coding component injects adversarial instructions via runtime feedback](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38799v1/framework_Crop.png)

*Figure 1: CARI exploits the "authority mismatch" — a peer CI/backend component has authority to report its own state but not to direct the victim agent's files or shell. The victim reads feedback during debugging and acts on embedded instructions.*

Runtime feedback from peer components (backend services, CI jobs, test harnesses) is a practical injection channel: across 10 attacks, four programming languages, and five models, 2,845 of 3,000 trials succeeded (94.8%). Product-level agents were also vulnerable: 61.3% on Codex and 58.7% on Claude Code. Generic prompt-level warnings were ineffective; explicit policy prompts suppressed attacks but substantially over-refused legitimate actions, revealing a utility-safety tradeoff with no clean solution.

### 05 · [**Understanding and Mitigating Role Spoofing in LLMs**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40396)

`preprint` `security` `mech-interp` — Ezra Edelman, Benjamin Edelman, Surbhi Goel, Eran Malach — arXiv cs.CR/cs.AI preprint, 29 Oct 2026

![Figure 2: Role attribution decomposition — models infer speaker roles from both content cues and structural formatting](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.40396v1/figures/bars_injection_decomposition_small.png)

*Figure 2: Models use two independent axes — content style and structural formatting — to infer who said what. Adversarial training against content attacks leaves structural role-spoofing fully intact.*

LLMs attribute text to conversational roles using both content-based and structure-based cues, allowing malicious tool outputs to impersonate the user via "role spoofing." Adversarial training that closes content-based injection leaves models fully exposed to structure-based attacks, which may explain why frontier models remain susceptible despite wide defenses. Linear probes trained to predict role labels can detect role spoofing effectively across tested settings, pointing to a lightweight detection approach.

### 06 · [**Scan Before You Install: Detecting and Mitigating Local and Composite Vulnerabilities in Skills**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37901)

`preprint` `security` — Parth A. Gandhi, Avital Aviv, Prasanna N. Wudali, Yuval Elovici, Asaf Shabtai — arXiv cs.CR/cs.AI preprint, 27 Oct 2026

![Figure 1: Skill anatomy — the six addressable components (instructions, scripts, reference files, invocations, shared resources, config)](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.37901v1/figures/skill-anatomy.png)

*Figure 1: A skill bundle's six components, each a potential vulnerability surface, analyzed in isolation (local) and as a dependency graph (composite).*

A six-module scanner — extraction, local analysis, composite analysis (dependency-graph modeling of shared resources and invocations), PoC validation, reporting, mitigation — achieves 95.90% recall with 4.22% FPR on SkillTrustBench (556 cases) and F1 of 0.947 on MalSkillBench (1,000 balanced), outperforming Cisco Skill Scanner, NVIDIA SkillSpector, and AgentSkillsScanner. Deployed against 1,587 real GitHub skills (75 top-starred repos), three analysts confirmed 53 of 59 findings (89.8% precision) across 23 repositories, and mitigations blocked PoC attacks in 47 of 55 cases.

### 08 · [**Activation Probes Are Reliable With a Handful of Positive Examples**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39766)

`NeurIPS 2025 Workshop` `mech-interp` — Riya Tyagi, Stefan Heimersheim — **NeurIPS 2025 Mechanistic Interpretability Workshop**

![Figure 1: Probe AUC vs. positive example count on Enron-Spam — few-shot vs. balanced training regimes](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.39766v1/viz/gemma_joint_compressed.png)

*Figure 1: Probe AUC on spam detection as a function of positive training examples (1–10). Training with many negatives (few-shot setting) consistently outperforms balanced training at low positive-sample counts.*

For monitoring rare misalignment, positive examples (unsafe behaviors) are scarce while negatives (safe behaviors) are abundant. The authors show that activation probes trained in this "few vs. thousands" regime outperform balanced training at 1–10 positive examples on spam and honesty detection tasks. LLM upsampling of positives gives a boost roughly equivalent to doubling the real positive count, but excessive upsampling degrades performance. Larger models are more positive-sample-efficient to probe — a positive scaling trend implying that misalignment monitoring should become cheaper at frontier scale.

### 09 · [**Appreciation Teaches Unauthorized Execution: Self-Evolution Degrades the Robustness of Personal Agents to Indirect Prompt Injection**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.39122)

`preprint` `security` — Xinyan Wang, Xiaogeng Liu, Jun Shao, Chaowei Xiao — arXiv cs.CR/cs.AI preprint, 29 Oct 2026

![Figure 1: Authorization conflation — after skill evolution, agents treat third-party content with the same authority as user instructions](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.39122v1/cartoon.png)

*Figure 1: Self-evolution conflates authorization: the agent learns to trust the style of third-party external content from prior user-approved tasks, enabling indirect prompt injection from new untrusted sources.*

Personal agents that learn from user feedback become substantially more susceptible to indirect prompt injection: skill evolution raised IPI attack success rate by 14.0–48.8 percentage points over the base agent; RL-based adaptation raised it by 3.0–39.3 points. The mechanism is authorization conflation — the agent generalizes trust from user-endorsed tasks to structurally similar third-party content. The result warns that self-improvement loops in deployed personal agents introduce a new attack surface orthogonal to prompt-level defenses.

*Figure 1: COP projects each gradient update onto the orthogonal complement of activation subspaces cached from all prior forget sets, storing only a low-rank basis per layer. Sequential updates are first-order non-interfering.*

Standard gradient-based unlearning collapses under sequential deletion requests — each new update partially reverts earlier erasures or damages general capability. Continual Orthogonal Projection (COP) restricts each update to the orthogonal complement of activation subspaces from previous forget sets, keeping only a compact low-rank basis per layer (no stored gradients or past data). On TOFU and RWKU benchmarks, COP preserves utility across sequential requests, maintains forgetting quality, and outperforms baselines designed specifically for continual unlearning.
