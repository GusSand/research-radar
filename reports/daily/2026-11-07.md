## 01 · [**SAVeR²: Reasoning-based Safety Alignment for Large Reasoning Models via Verifiable Rewards**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07482)

`alignment` `safety training` `reasoning LLMs` `preprint` — Yichen Sun, Qirui Liu, Yan Wang, Jianan Lin, Linbo Jiang, Shiyu Wang, Zhibo Wang, Zhixuan Chu — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Large Reasoning Models expose a sharp new failure mode: a model can reason its way into an unsafe answer, and existing safety alignment methods either impose a "safety tax" (degrading the chain-of-thought) or generalize poorly to adversarial inputs. SAVeR² closes this gap with a training framework purpose-built for reasoning LLMs that ties safety rewards directly to verifiable properties of the reasoning chain.

![Figure 2: SAVeR² overview showing the four verifiable reward components, difficulty-aware data selection, and reasoning-preserving gradient projection](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07482v1/overview1.png)

*Figure 2: SAVeR² splits the safety objective into four verifiable reward signals—safety reasoning, non-maliciousness, reasoning–answer consistency, and usefulness—then projects the safety gradient to avoid conflicting with the reasoning gradient.*

The method has three components: (1) a **safety reasoning warm-up** that fine-tunes on refusal traces following an Assess → Identify → Justify → Refuse → Redirect pattern; (2) **difficulty-aware data selection** using Gaussian-weighted sampling centered on a scale-dependent target difficulty; (3) **reasoning-preserving gradient projection (GRPO)** that eliminates first-order conflicts between safety and reasoning gradients. At 7B parameters, average safety score reaches 92.80 vs. the best baseline at 83.50 (PPO); at 14B, 94.28 vs. 87.22 (STAR-1). Under adversarial prompts (GCG/PAIR/TAP on AdvBench), SAVeR²-14B achieves 97.33% defense success—highest of four compared methods. The 671B variant reaches 98.80 Safe@1, near Claude-4-Sonnet's 98.98, while raising reasoning Think@1 from 37.98 (base) to 72.99.

## 02 · [**J-Anchor: Defending Against Prefill Attacks via J-Space Selective Refusal Restoration**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01342)

`safety` `jailbreak defense` `prefill attack` `preprint` — Running Yang, Beidi Zhao, Xiaoxiao Li — arXiv preprint, Oct 2026

Prefill jailbreaks force a model to continue a harmful assistant prefix that the model would normally refuse. This paper diagnoses the precise mechanism through the Jacobian lens and fixes it without any additional training.

![Figure 1: J-Anchor mechanism showing refusal-state erosion under prefill attack and restoration via J-space anchoring](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.01342v1/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Under a prefill attack the refusal signal collapses to the benign baseline while the harmfulness signal stays elevated—J-Anchor uses the pre-attack assistant-header state as an anchor to restore the depleted refusal components.*

Using the Jacobian (J-lens), the authors show that prefill attacks cause **refusal-state erosion**: the refusal signal declines to the benign baseline by the end of the prefill while the harmfulness signal remains elevated. J-Anchor is a training-free, inference-time fix that (1) records the pre-attack refusal state at the assistant header as a request-specific anchor, (2) gates intervention on a risk threshold (τ = 0.75), and (3) rescales and injects the anchor's refusal components into the generation stream. On fixed-prefix attacks, StrongREJECT ASR falls from 65.2% → 1.0% (Llama-3.1-8B), 82.7% → 0.0% (Gemma-4-E4B-IT), 77.3% → 0.3% (Qwen3.5-4B). Deep prefill (k=500) is similarly neutralized: ASR ≤ 2.5% across all three backbones. Benign over-refusal on XSTest remains within 4 pp of the clean baseline.

## 03 · [**Forget What Comes Next: LLM Unlearning via Statistical Independence**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03955)

`alignment` `machine unlearning` `mech-interp` `preprint` — Andrii Shkabrii, Claudia Plant, Martin Pawelczyk — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Standard unlearning methods can erase outputs without erasing what the model actually knows—poisoned training examples can remain statistically detectable even after "unlearning." PINT (Pruning via Independence Training) attacks this at the representation level using information-theoretic tools.

![Figure 2: PINT overview showing HSIC-based independence minimization between prefix representations and forgotten tokens](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03955v1/pint-overview_cropped.png)

*Figure 2: PINT minimizes HSIC between prefix representations and next tokens on the forget set, coupled with a final-layer representation-matching retain loss.*

PINT minimizes the **Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC)** between prefix representations and their predicted next tokens on the forget set (Eq. 1), provably bounding Exact Memorization leakage (Theorem 3.2). A retain loss (Eq. 2) anchors final-layer representations on retain data close to the original model. Average area under the forgetting-utility Pareto frontier: PINT 0.95, vs. ≤0.90 for all baselines. On Gaussian poison detection, PINT makes the poison undetectable while retaining 91% utility; the best baseline retains only 63%. Scales to Llama-3-70B: WMDP-Cyber drops from 53.0% → 27.6% (target ≈ 25%) while MMLU falls only 0.002 points.

## 04 · [**SafeStart: Mitigating the Overlooked Safety Cost of Active Expert Reduction in MoE LLM Serving**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06147)

`safety` `deployment` `MoE` `preprint` — Erfan Baghaei Potraghloo, Saeid Shokoufa, Seyedarmin Azizi, Arya Fayyazi, Souvik Kundu, Mehdi Kamal, Massoud Pedram — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Speed optimizations for Mixture-of-Experts models are routinely validated on accuracy benchmarks but not on safety benchmarks—this paper shows that omission is dangerous: running fewer experts substantially weakens refusal of harmful requests.

![Figure 1: SafeStart results showing attack success rates under expert reduction vs. SafeStart defense across three affected MoE models](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06147/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Expert reduction at two-thirds of selected experts raises harmful compliance by 18.4 points on Qwen1.5-MoE, 9.5 on OLMoE, and 6.6 on DeepSeek-V2-Lite; SafeStart restores safety with ≤1.3 point change.*

The authors test nine MoE models in vLLM 0.11.0 on HarmBench and StrongREJECT with and without jailbreak templates. Using ~2/3 of experts yields 15–28% decode speedup at batch size 8 but raises attack success by 18.4 points (Qwen1.5-MoE), 9.5 (OLMoE), and 6.6 (DeepSeek-V2-Lite). The fix—**SafeStart**—runs all experts for the prompt plus the first 8 generated tokens, then switches to the reduced budget. It is a Triton kernel patch requiring no model changes. Under the same jailbreak, change in harmful compliance becomes −0.6 to +1.3 pp while retaining ≥85% of the speedup at batch sizes 8 and 32. The finding implies that all MoE serving optimizations should be validated against safety benchmarks before production deployment.

## 05 · [**Multimodal-SEA: Benchmarking AI Agent Security under Multimodal Social Engineering Attacks**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08281)

`security` `agent security` `benchmark` `social engineering` `preprint` — Zhitao He, Chi Him Zhang, Chengru Han, Yi R. Fung — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Personal AI agents are increasingly targeted by social engineering attacks that exploit multimodal content (forged images, documents, websites) to deceive agents into malicious actions—but no prior benchmark tested agents in realistic multimodal fraud scenarios.

![Figure 1: Multimodal-SEA task execution environment showing 50 simulated apps, attack harness, and 6-dimensional evaluation](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08281v1/task_execution.png)

*Figure 1: Agents operate in an interactive environment with 50 apps and historical user records; a DeepSeek-v4-Pro attacker executes multi-stage scripts with forged multimodal content.*

Multimodal-SEA provides 608 expert-designed tasks from real-world fraud cases, spanning 152 attack scenarios (79 Chinese, 73 English) across 14 domains with 4 difficulty levels. Outcomes are verified against tool logs and environment state. All seven multimodal models tested are vulnerable: pooled attack success rate 7.1%–34.5%. Adding multimodal content raises average ASR from 19.9% → 29.2% even as proactive verification coverage improves (54.7% → 65.1%), revealing that agents verify more but still miss deceptive content. Deception recognition miss rates reach 70.9% (English tasks). Safety prompts halved attack success on GPT-5.6-Sol but increased multimodal deception miss rate from 50.4% → 63.5%. Claude Opus 5 had the lowest ASR (7.1%) but refused 32.1% of legitimate benign requests.

## 06 · [**Decision Models as Retrieval Gates Against Injection and Pollution**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08325)

`security` `prompt injection` `RAG security` `preprint` — Mahdi Ghadamyari, Reza Habibi — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Adding a small decision model that answers typed yes/no questions about retrieved passages can cut prompt injection attack success in half and nearly eliminate retrieval pollution—with under 80 ms of latency per passage.

![Figure 1: Pipeline showing decision model gate on retrieved passages before the main LLM generator](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08325v1/figures/fig1_pipeline.png)

*Figure 1: Retrieved passages are screened by a Kev-4B decision model before reaching the main LLM; the gate detects injected instructions and synthetic pollution pages independently.*

On 1,000 questions over forum pages with injected instructions, attack success falls from 27.9% (baseline) → 13.6% (published mitigations only) → **5.7%** (with Kev-4B gate added). The gate takes **76 ms** per passage and misfires on only 4 of 186 benign instruction-containing pages. On retrieval pollution (200 queries, 2/3 fake pages), filtering the full candidate pool before top-k selection holds accuracy at 83% and limits fake-page exposure to 0.5% vs. 28% accuracy with no filter. Reading HTML rather than plain text further improves attack detection. Large LLM judges achieve better AUROC (0.99 vs. 0.94) but cost ~10× more—the decision model is the practical choice.

## 07 · [**Auditable Unlearning for LLMs**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05866)

`alignment` `machine unlearning` `evaluation` `preprint` — Piyush Tiwary, Anushka Srivastava, Prathosh A.P. — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Machine unlearning can claim to have deleted data while leaving the information statistically recoverable—this paper adds "auditability" as a fourth evaluation axis alongside forget quality, retain quality, and membership-inference resistance, and shows no existing method satisfies all four.

![Figure 1: GapWiden-GapVeil framework showing audit gap widening and inference-time rejection sampling](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.05866/x1.png)

*Figure 1: GapWiden uses a hinge loss to separate forget-set from retain-set audit statistics post-unlearning; GapVeil applies an inference-time rejection sampler to suppress responses with high audit statistics.*

Auditability is measured by how well a provider's statistic (token-level log-probability gap between base model and unlearned model) separates forget-set from retain-set examples (AUROC). Across 11 methods on TOFU, MUSE, and RWKU, none achieves all four axes. The two-stage fix: **GapWiden** (post-training hinge loss, margin m=6) widens the audit gap; **GapVeil** (inference-time rejection sampler within K draws) suppresses leakage at query time. With both stages, auditability and leakage reach oracle parity for every method tested on TOFU. On MUSE, average auditability improves from 0.84→0.99 (News) and 0.81→0.99 (Books). Attacker advantage with GapVeil falls into a 0.11–0.15 band vs. 0.10–0.37 without it.

## 08 · [**Decodability Is Not Validity: Evaluating Nuisance Sensitivity in Language Model Concept Probes**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07805)

`mech-interp` `probing` `evaluation methodology` `preprint` — Ayal Klein — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Two probes can have identical accuracy at decoding a concept from an LLM's hidden states yet respond very differently to a correlated nuisance attribute—making standard decodability metrics dangerous proxies for probe reliability.

![Figure 1: Scatter plot of test decodability vs. counterfactual nuisance sensitivity for competitive extractors showing the absence of correlation](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.07805/x1.png)

*Figure 1: Competitive extractors (within 0.05 Dec of the best) show that rank correlation between decodability and nuisance sensitivity (CFN) changes sign across four target–nuisance settings.*

The study covers four target–nuisance settings on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9B-it: CEBaB (service vs. food sentiment), BiasBios (surgeon vs. nurse with gender as nuisance), and two Amazon settings (negation and first-person framing). Under correlated training data, extractors with statistically equivalent held-out AUROC differ up to several-fold in counterfactual nuisance sensitivity in three of four settings. The effect follows the probe's training data, not the natural association: first-person framing (uncorrelated with sentiment in natural reviews) is absorbed at least as much as negation. Practical fix: selecting extractors using **nuisance-aware development diagnostics** (Conflict score) lowers CFN wherever the default choice is sensitive at a mean accuracy cost of ≤0.012 Dec. Direct implication for safety probes: a probe that looks valid by standard metrics may be detecting a superficial correlate rather than the target concept.

## 09 · [**Signed but Steerable: Measuring and Closing the Text-Vector Integrity Gap in Offline Retrieval-Augmented Generation**](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06498)

`security` `RAG` `integrity` `preprint` — Georgios Feretzakis, Alexandros Papaspyridis — arXiv preprint, Nov 2026

Offline RAG knowledge packs carry pre-computed embeddings alongside text—a valid signature proves provenance but not that the vectors match the text, leaving a window for an attacker to swap vectors while passing integrity verification.

![Figure 1: Text-vector integrity gap in offline RAG showing how a cosine-tolerance verification scheme can be bypassed by targeted vector manipulation](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06498/x1.png)

*Figure 1: An attacker manipulates embedding vectors within the tolerance band of honest cosines, causing the retrieval system to surface attacker-controlled documents while passing signature verification.*

On a FAISS PQ index over 57,638 documents, tampered vectors passing audit ranked the attacker's document first for **25% of targets**. With an idealized tolerance at floating-point noise level, up to 5.5% of queries remain exposed. On a Raspberry Pi 5 running a local LLM, the attack drops correct answers from 0.75 → 0.39. The proposed fix verifies **quantization codes** (with a calibrated guard band and canonicalization) rather than raw cosines; it accepted every honest vector in their samples across three platforms. Notably, common dynamic-INT8 inference pipelines are not bit-reproducible across batch sizes or architectures, complicating any floating-point-level check.

## 10 · [**METR: Independent Assessment of AI R&D Acceleration at Anthropic in October 2026**](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/)

`alignment` `dangerous capabilities` `AI development` `lab blog` — METR (Zach Stein-Perlman et al.) — METR public report, 5 Nov 2026

METR conducted a high-access evaluation at Anthropic from August to October 2026 to independently assess whether AI is now meaningfully accelerating AI R&D—a threshold that directly bears on safety planning under Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy.

The assessment found that Anthropic's progress on well-specified tasks is running at approximately **1.9–2× its March 2024–October 2025 pace**, with a range of 1.3×–3× not ruled out. METR attributed a majority of this acceleration to AI being used in AI R&D itself, though internal Anthropic staff did not reach consensus on this attribution. Self-reported Slack polls indicated ~12× individual productivity uplift, which METR treated as only loose supporting evidence. The public version had a majority of the most informative evidence redacted by Anthropic; METR notes readers must rely more on its judgment than METR would prefer. The report implies Anthropic's RSP "dramatic acceleration" threshold may already have been crossed—a finding with direct consequences for safety commitments and development pace.
